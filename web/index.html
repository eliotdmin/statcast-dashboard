What “% real” means — and what it does not

The definition. For a metric over n plate appearances, % real is ρ = var(true) / var(observed): of all the spread between players in this percentile column, the share that comes from players genuinely differing rather than from the luck of which n pitches landed in the window. It is a property of the column, not of any one player. “45% real” does not mean this hitter’s wOBA is 45% accurate; it means that re-running the same six weeks would scramble about half the ranking.

Where it comes from. Odd half-month blocks are correlated against even ones, corrected for the split by Spearman–Brown (ρ₀ = 2r/(1+r)), then projected to any other sample size by the same formula run forward: ρ(n) = kρ₀/(1+(k−1)ρ₀) with k = n/n₀. The projection predicts a month-to-month wOBA reliability of .15 against .135 measured independently, and bat speed .94 against .919 — close enough to trust the shape, not the third decimal.

Why the bar is drawn twice. The honest estimate of a player’s true percentile is the raw one pulled toward the league at exactly the rate the metric is unreliable: 50 + ρ×(raw−50). That is the solid bar; the dashed outline is what an uncorrected page would print. That the shrinkage coefficient equals the reliability is not a convention — E[T|X] = μ + b(X−μ) with b = cov(T,X)/var(X) = var(T)/var(X) = ρ.

Biggest moves in the league

Two different questions, and you need both answers

z asks: could noise have done this? The change is divided by the standard error that sampling noise alone would produce across these two sample sizes. var_noise(n) = var_league(n)×(1−ρ(n)), and noise scales as 1/n, so se = sqrt(var_noise(n_now) + var_noise(n_prior)).

sd asks: would anyone notice? The same change divided by how much players differ from each other. These come apart in both directions. Arm angle is measured with reliability .999, so a 0.9° drift clears z = 2.5 and is 0.07 sd — unambiguous and meaningless. Going the other way, a strikeout rate that falls four points over 240 batters faced can be 0.6 sd and still sit inside what chance produces; the honest sentence is “he may well have changed this much and this sample cannot tell you.”

held is the out-of-sample check. It is the share of the move still present in the following window. Across 2026, 47% of moves past |z| = 2 held at least half. The window-over-window correlation of these deltas runs from +.37 (K%) to +.58 (attack angle), so a move that repeats is worth considerably more than a move that does not.

How the expectation is built, and what failed on the way

The model. Next window’s wOBA is regressed on four things: the player’s wOBA and xwOBA in the current window, and his wOBA and xwOBA over every strictly prior block of the same season. Fit on 2023–2025, evaluated once on 2026. The baseline never contains the future, which is the difference between a forecast and a description.

Read R² against the ceiling, never on its own. The target is itself a noisy measurement, so even a perfect forecast of true talent scores badly. R²_max = var(T)/var(T+e) with var(e) = E[σ²/n] and σ² = 0.2258 per plate appearance. The numbers appear in the tiles above.

What failed. The intended product was “streaks the body corroborates will carry; streaks it does not will not.” A reliability-weighted composite of input changes adds a coefficient worth 0.004 of wOBA per standard deviation and no measurable R² at 15-, 30- or 45-day resolution. Splitting hot streaks by corroboration moves the carry rate 27% → 32%, CI [−12%, +22%], P(gap>0) = 0.73. Retracted before it was built. The paradox worth sitting with: those input changes do persist (r ≈ .5) and still do not predict next month’s wOBA, because next month’s wOBA is barely predictable by anything.

Spearman–Brown, run backwards

Reliability mode solves ρ(n) = t for n: k = t(1−p) / (p(1−t)), n = k×n₀. Note the (1−t) in the denominator — the price of the last few points of reliability is unbounded. Going from .70 to .90 is not 1.3× the sample, it is 3.9×.

Detection mode is a power calculation on the same decomposition. Noise variance at sample n is (1−p)×var(n₀)×(n₀/n), so against a baseline large enough to treat as known, n = n₀(1−p)×var(n₀)×((zα/2+zβ)/δ)². A finite baseline of m gives n = C/(1−C/m), and when C ≥ m there is no follow-up window that resolves the difference — the answer is to lengthen the baseline first, not to watch longer.

Why this belongs before the intervention, not after. Every clinical trial computes its sample size in the protocol; post-hoc power analysis is treated as an error. A hitting lab that changes a swing and evaluates it on forty swings has run an underpowered trial — forty swings resolves bat speed fine and resolves essentially nothing about exit velocity, hard-hit rate or outcomes.

Half-month cutoffs on the 1st and 16th; the browser holds per-block counts and sums whichever range you pick, so no range is precomputed. Percentiles are against players with a comparable workload in the same span and season. Reliability at a given sample size is projected from the measured half-season split-half via Spearman–Brown, which assumes true talent held still inside the window and is therefore mildly optimistic over long stretches.
