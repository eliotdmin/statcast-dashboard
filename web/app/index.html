What this view is for Judging a stretch you already care about. You have a player and a date range; this says where he ranked among his peers over exactly those dates, and how much of that ranking to believe.

Read the solid bar, not the dashed one. The dashed outline is what a Savant-style page prints. The solid bar is what survives once you account for how much of that column is noise at this sample size, and the gap between them is the part nobody can vouch for. A hitter in the 98th percentile of wOBA over six weeks routinely shrinks into the 70s; a hitter in the 98th percentile of bat speed stays there.

Using it to pick players. When the body numbers and the results disagree, back the body at any sample shorter than a full season — that is not a preference, it is what the % real chips are measuring. The groups are ordered deliberately: what he does with the bat is nearly fully real inside a fortnight, plate discipline needs a month or two, and Results needs a season and a half before the ranking means much. For a waiver claim or a trade, the useful question is not “who is hot” but “whose solid bars are high while his name is cheap.”

The comps below answer a different question: who else looks like this right now. Distance is weighted by reliability at your sample size, so over a short window it leans almost entirely on the swing and ignores the results — which is the correct behaviour and why the matches often have very different batting lines.

What “% real” means — and what it does not

The definition. For a metric over n plate appearances, % real is ρ = var(true) / var(observed): of all the spread between players in this percentile column, the share that comes from players genuinely differing rather than from the luck of which n pitches landed in the window. It is a property of the column, not of any one player. “45% real” does not mean this hitter’s wOBA is 45% accurate; it means that re-running the same six weeks would scramble about half the ranking.

Where it comes from. Odd half-month blocks are correlated against even ones, corrected for the split by Spearman–Brown (ρ₀ = 2r/(1+r)), then projected to any other sample size by the same formula run forward: ρ(n) = kρ₀/(1+(k−1)ρ₀) with k = n/n₀. The projection predicts a month-to-month wOBA reliability of .15 against .135 measured independently, and bat speed .94 against .919 — close enough to trust the shape, not the third decimal.

Why the bar is drawn twice. The honest estimate of a player’s true percentile is the raw one pulled toward the league at exactly the rate the metric is unreliable: 50 + ρ×(raw−50). That is the solid bar; the dashed outline is what an uncorrected page would print. That the shrinkage coefficient equals the reliability is not a convention — E[T|X] = μ + b(X−μ) with b = cov(T,X)/var(X) = var(T)/var(X) = ρ.

What this view is for Finding the moment a player actually changed something, as distinct from the moment his numbers changed. Every row compares one window against that player’s own earlier season and divides by the error two windows of that size produce on their own.

This is the earliest honest signal on the site. Bat speed, swing length and attack angle are better than 90% reliable inside a fortnight, so a genuine mechanical change surfaces here weeks before it reaches a box score. That lead time is the entire edge: by the time a swing change shows up in wOBA, the player is no longer cheap.

held is the column that matters. It is the share of the move still present in the following window, computed after the fact — an out-of-sample check rather than a promise. Across 2026, 47% of moves past |z| = 2 held at least half of themselves. A confirmed move on attack angle or bat speed is the most actionable row on this page; an unconfirmed one is a watch-list entry, not a claim.

Using it for a fantasy roster. Filter to improved, confirmed only, and a swing metric, and you have a short list of hitters whose mechanics measurably moved and stayed moved. Cross-check each against the Percentiles tab: a real attack-angle gain on a hitter whose bat speed is already in the 90th percentile is a different proposition from the same gain on someone who cannot drive the ball. Run the same filter with declined to find the players to move off before the results catch up.

Biggest moves in the league

Two different questions, and you need both answers

z asks: could noise have done this? The change is divided by the standard error that sampling noise alone would produce across these two sample sizes. var_noise(n) = var_league(n)×(1−ρ(n)), and noise scales as 1/n, so se = sqrt(var_noise(n_now) + var_noise(n_prior)).

sd asks: would anyone notice? The same change divided by how much players differ from each other. These come apart in both directions. Arm angle is measured with reliability .999, so a 0.9° drift clears z = 2.5 and is 0.07 sd — unambiguous and meaningless. Going the other way, a strikeout rate that falls four points over 240 batters faced can be 0.6 sd and still sit inside what chance produces; the honest sentence is “he may well have changed this much and this sample cannot tell you.”

held is the out-of-sample check. It is the share of the move still present in the following window. Across 2026, 47% of moves past |z| = 2 held at least half. The window-over-window correlation of these deltas runs from +.37 (K%) to +.58 (attack angle), so a move that repeats is worth considerably more than a move that does not.

What this view is for Deciding whether to buy or sell a streak. Every qualified player is sorted by how far his recent results sit from his own earlier-season rate, next to how much of that gap the model expects to survive the next month.

carry is a discount rate, not a prediction. A hitter 240 points above his own baseline with 32% carry is still expected to be better than that baseline going forward — by roughly 77 points, not 240. Nobody is being told to regress to league average.

Where the trades are. Sell candidates are a large positive gap with low carry: the market is pricing a stretch the model does not believe. Buy candidates are the mirror — a large negative gap with low carry is a cold streak that is mostly not real, and those players are cheap for a reason that will not persist. Two rows with the same gap can have very different carry, and the difference is almost entirely whether the expected-stats line moved with the results or stayed put.

Read the R² tiles honestly. The model captures about 12% of the forecastable variance that exists at this horizon, which sounds bad until you see the ceiling tile next to it: next month’s wOBA is mostly noise, so a perfect forecaster scores 0.285, not 1.0. The tile on the right shows what last month’s results alone are worth — close to nothing, which is the reason this page exists.

How the expectation is built, and what failed on the way

The model. Next window’s wOBA is regressed on four things: the player’s wOBA and xwOBA in the current window, and his wOBA and xwOBA over every strictly prior block of the same season. Fit on 2023–2025, evaluated once on 2026. The baseline never contains the future, which is the difference between a forecast and a description.

Read R² against the ceiling, never on its own. The target is itself a noisy measurement, so even a perfect forecast of true talent scores badly. R²_max = var(T)/var(T+e) with var(e) = E[σ²/n] and σ² = 0.2258 per plate appearance. The numbers appear in the tiles above.

What failed. The intended product was “streaks the body corroborates will carry; streaks it does not will not.” A reliability-weighted composite of input changes adds a coefficient worth 0.004 of wOBA per standard deviation and no measurable R² at 15-, 30- or 45-day resolution. Splitting hot streaks by corroboration moves the carry rate 27% → 32%, CI [−12%, +22%], P(gap>0) = 0.73. Retracted before it was built. The paradox worth sitting with: those input changes do persist (r ≈ .5) and still do not predict next month’s wOBA, because next month’s wOBA is barely predictable by anything.

What this view is for Deciding how much data a question needs before you go and collect it. Everywhere else on this site answers “what does this number mean.” This one answers “how long until this number means anything.”

Reliability mode — the first two tiles and the table. Pick a metric and a target, and it solves Spearman–Brown backwards for the sample size at which the ranking becomes that real. Bat speed reaches 70% at 13 plate appearances; wOBA needs 525, and 2,023 to reach 90%. A season is about 600, so a wOBA leaderboard never becomes a 90%-real ranking inside one. The curve plots the whole relationship, with your target marked.

Detection mode — the “change to detect” box. A different question, asked by a coach rather than an analyst: not is the ranking real but he changed something in the cage, how long until I know it took. It is a standard power calculation with the noise variance supplied by the measured reliability. A pitcher who has lost 1.0 mph is confirmable in 26 batters faced, one start. A hitter who has gained 1.0 mph of bat speed needs 369 plate appearances.

The case worth knowing about. Set a baseline size and the answer can come back impossible — when the baseline itself is too noisy, no follow-up window of any length resolves the difference, and the fix is to lengthen the baseline rather than watch longer. Any hitting lab comparing a post-change stretch against three weeks of “before” is in that regime and cannot get out of it by collecting more “after.”

Spearman–Brown, run backwards

Reliability mode solves ρ(n) = t for n: k = t(1−p) / (p(1−t)), n = k×n₀. Note the (1−t) in the denominator — the price of the last few points of reliability is unbounded. Going from .70 to .90 is not 1.3× the sample, it is 3.9×.

Detection mode is a power calculation on the same decomposition. Noise variance at sample n is (1−p)×var(n₀)×(n₀/n), so against a baseline large enough to treat as known, n = n₀(1−p)×var(n₀)×((zα/2+zβ)/δ)². A finite baseline of m gives n = C/(1−C/m), and when C ≥ m there is no follow-up window that resolves the difference — the answer is to lengthen the baseline first, not to watch longer.

Why this belongs before the intervention, not after. Every clinical trial computes its sample size in the protocol; post-hoc power analysis is treated as an error. A hitting lab that changes a swing and evaluates it on forty swings has run an underpowered trial — forty swings resolves bat speed fine and resolves essentially nothing about exit velocity, hard-hit rate or outcomes.
