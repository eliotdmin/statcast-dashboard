Is his recent form real?

Applies to this analysis

What this answers Whether to believe what one player has done lately, pulling the other views into a single read.

How to read it

Hot or cold takes two tests. His recent stretch has to differ clearly from both his rate earlier this season and his usual level from prior seasons (up to three, the latest weighted most, pulled toward league average by how much he has played). A star climbing out of a slow start fails the second test — that is a return to form, and the card says so. A player with no prior MLB seasons is measured against the league average with wide uncertainty, so it takes a bigger move to call him either way.

How the verdict works. It compares his most recent stretch with everything earlier this season, then asks how much of the difference is likely to stick, using the Streak carry forecast model and how similar streaks have gone. It is deliberately conservative: across 2023–2025, two-week hot streaks kept only 14% of their gain and six-week ones 43%, so a hot stretch is never called a breakout.

What it can’t tell you

Why a swing change doesn’t move it. A real change in bat speed or chase rate is worth knowing and is listed on the card, but on this project’s evidence it does not predict next month’s results, so it is never used to make the call.

It will not tell you to buy or sell. By rule the verdict is conservative and never issues a recommendation: it reports whether a stretch clears the bar, and leaves the decision where it belongs.

How the shrinkage works The definition of “% real,” where it comes from, and how each bar is adjusted for sample size.

The definition. For a metric over n plate appearances, % real is ρ = var(true) / var(observed): of all the spread between players in this percentile column, the share that comes from players genuinely differing rather than from the luck of which n pitches landed in the window. It is a property of the column, not of any one player. “45% real” does not mean this hitter’s wOBA is 45% accurate; it means that re-running the same six weeks would scramble about half the ranking.

Where it comes from. Odd half-month blocks are correlated against even ones, corrected for the split by Spearman–Brown (ρ₀ = 2r/(1+r)), then projected to any other sample size by the same formula run forward: ρ(n) = kρ₀/(1+(k−1)ρ₀) with k = n/n₀. The projection predicts a month-to-month wOBA reliability of .15 against .135 measured independently, and bat speed .94 against .919 — close enough to trust the shape, not the third decimal.

How the bar is adjusted. The honest estimate of a player’s true percentile is the raw one pulled toward the league at exactly the rate the metric is unreliable: 50 + ρ×(raw−50). That is the solid bar; the hatched extension reaches to what an uncorrected page would print, and is only drawn when the two differ by five points or more. That the shrinkage coefficient equals the reliability is not a convention — E[T|X] = μ + b(X−μ) with b = cov(T,X)/var(X) = var(T)/var(X) = ρ.

What this answers Where a player actually ranked over a date range you pick — and how much of that ranking is real rather than luck.

How to read it

Read the solid bar. It is the percentile after accounting for how much of that column is noise at this sample size. Where that adjustment moved the number by five points or more, the hatched extension shows how far the raw figure a Savant-style page would print reaches past it — the part nobody can vouch for. A hitter in the 98th percentile of wOBA over six weeks routinely shrinks into the 70s; a hitter in the 98th percentile of bat speed stays there.

Using it to pick players. When the body numbers and the results disagree, back the body at any sample shorter than a full season — that is not a preference, it is what the % real chips are measuring. The groups are ordered deliberately: what he does with the bat is nearly fully real inside a fortnight, plate discipline needs a month or two, and Results needs a season and a half before the ranking means much. For a waiver claim or a trade, the useful question is not “who is hot” but “whose solid bars are high while his name is cheap.”

The comps below answer a different question: who else looks like this right now. Distance is weighted by reliability at your sample size, so over a short window it leans almost entirely on the swing and ignores the results — which is the correct behaviour and why the matches often have very different batting lines.

What it can’t tell you

It is a report card, not a forecast. Every bar describes days that have already been played. Nothing here says whether he will keep it up — that is what Streak carry forecast and Head-to-head odds are for.

The peer group is not all of baseball. Percentiles are against players with comparable playing time over a comparable span, so “90th percentile” means 90th among those men, not among everyone who took a plate appearance.

What this answers Which of two players is likelier to finish ahead over a window you choose, given as odds rather than as a projected stat line.

How to read it

Read the split, not just the number. The “Why the odds are this close to 50%” bar divides the uncertainty three ways. Talent is how little we know about what these two players are. Drift is the fact that what they are will change over the window. Sampling is plain luck over the plate appearances ahead. Across every matchup in the backtest, sampling is about 63% of it and talent under 5% — which is why two clearly different players are still close to a coin flip over two weeks, and why the answer barely improves when you stretch the window to the rest of the season: sampling noise falls as 1/n, but drift accumulates.

The calibration table is the receipt. It is not a claim about this matchup; it is what happened to every matchup the same estimator has ever been asked, scored against what the players actually did next. If it says 62% and the row for 62% reads 63%, the number on this page means what it says.

What it can’t tell you

Why not a projection. A one-month point forecast is worth very little here and this project has measured exactly how little: Marcel scores R² 0.058, a 48-feature Statcast model 0.057, and every gap between them straddles zero. Against a realistic ceiling of 0.264 that is about a fifth of what is knowable. Ordering a pair is a much easier question than estimating a level, because only the sign of the difference has to beat the noise — so that is the question this tab asks.

It knows nothing about injuries, demotions or a swing change. The drift term is a single league-average constant. If you know something the box scores don’t, the playing-time boxes are where you tell it.

The estimated gap is not a predicted margin. It is the difference between two shrunk estimates of true talent. Neither player will “hit” it.

Spearman–Brown, run backwards The formulas behind both modes, and why sample-size planning belongs before the intervention, not after.

Reliability mode solves ρ(n) = t for n: k = t(1−p) / (p(1−t)), n = k×n₀. Note the (1−t) in the denominator — the price of the last few points of reliability is unbounded. Going from .70 to .90 is not 1.3× the sample, it is 3.9×.

Detection mode is a power calculation on the same decomposition. Noise variance at sample n is (1−p)×var(n₀)×(n₀/n), so against a baseline large enough to treat as known, n = n₀(1−p)×var(n₀)×((zα/2+zβ)/δ)². A finite baseline of m gives n = C/(1−C/m), and when C ≥ m there is no follow-up window that resolves the difference — the answer is to lengthen the baseline first, not to watch longer.

Why this belongs before the intervention, not after. Every clinical trial computes its sample size in the protocol; post-hoc power analysis is treated as an error. A hitting lab that changes a swing and evaluates it on forty swings has run an underpowered trial — forty swings resolves bat speed fine and resolves essentially nothing about exit velocity, hard-hit rate or outcomes.

What this answers How much data a question needs, worked out before you go and collect it.

How to read it

Reliability mode — the first two tiles and the table. Pick a metric and a target, and it solves Spearman–Brown backwards for the sample size at which the ranking becomes that real. Bat speed reaches 70% at 13 plate appearances; wOBA needs 525, and 2,023 to reach 90%. A season is about 600, so a wOBA leaderboard never becomes a 90%-real ranking inside one. The curve plots the whole relationship, with your target marked.

Detection mode — the “change to detect” box. A different question, asked by a coach rather than an analyst: not is the ranking real but he changed something in the cage, how long until I know it took. It is a standard power calculation with the noise variance supplied by the measured reliability. A pitcher who has lost 1.0 mph is confirmable in 26 batters faced, one start. A hitter who has gained 1.0 mph of bat speed needs 369 plate appearances.

What it can’t tell you

The case worth knowing about. Set a baseline size and the answer can come back impossible — when the baseline itself is too noisy, no follow-up window of any length resolves the difference, and the fix is to lengthen the baseline rather than watch longer. Any hitting lab comparing a post-change stretch against three weeks of “before” is in that regime and cannot get out of it by collecting more “after.”

It assumes this player is an ordinary one. The spread it works from is the league’s, not his. For a genuine outlier the real answer is smaller than the one shown, and there is no way to know that in advance.

z, sd, and held — what each one is for Two different questions, and you need both answers, plus the out-of-sample check that decides whether a move is worth trusting.

z asks: could noise have done this? The change is divided by the standard error that sampling noise alone would produce across these two sample sizes. var_noise(n) = var_league(n)×(1−ρ(n)), and noise scales as 1/n, so se = sqrt(var_noise(n_now) + var_noise(n_prior)).

sd asks: would anyone notice? The same change divided by how much players differ from each other. These come apart in both directions. Arm angle is measured with reliability .999, so a 0.9° drift clears z = 2.5 and is 0.07 sd — unambiguous and meaningless. Going the other way, a strikeout rate that falls four points over 240 batters faced can be 0.6 sd and still sit inside what chance produces; the honest sentence is “he may well have changed this much and this sample cannot tell you.”

held is the out-of-sample check. It is the share of the move still present over the following 60 days, whatever the window length. Across 2026, 46% of 30-day moves past |z| = 2 held at least half (26% of 15-day moves, 58% of 45-day ones). The window-over-window correlation of these deltas runs from +.37 (K%) to +.58 (attack angle), so a move that repeats is worth considerably more than a move that does not.

What this answers Whether a player genuinely changed something, as opposed to his numbers simply moving around.

How to read it

This is the earliest honest signal on the site. Bat speed, swing length and attack angle are better than 90% reliable inside a fortnight, so a genuine mechanical change surfaces here weeks before it reaches a box score. That lead time is the entire edge: by the time a swing change shows up in wOBA, the player is no longer cheap.

held is the column that matters. It is the share of the move still present over the following 60 days, computed after the fact — an out-of-sample check rather than a promise. The follow-up is the same length for every window, because a follow-up as short as the window itself is too noisy to judge one row by. Across 2026, 46% of 30-day moves past |z| = 2 held at least half of themselves. Moves from the last 60 days of data have no verdict yet. A confirmed move on attack angle or bat speed is the most actionable row on this page; an unconfirmed one is a watch-list entry, not a claim.

Using it for a fantasy roster. Filter to improved, confirmed only, and a swing metric, and you have a short list of hitters whose mechanics measurably moved and stayed moved. Cross-check each against Luck-adjusted percentiles: a real attack-angle gain on a hitter whose bat speed is already in the 90th percentile is a different proposition from the same gain on someone who cannot drive the ball. Run the same filter with declined to find the players to move off before the results catch up.

How this differs from the Streak carry forecast. held looks back at one player’s actual change in his swing or approach and asks whether it stuck. The Streak carry forecast looks forward at his results and asks how much of a hot or cold streak to expect next month. A swing change can be real and still barely move next month’s results — which is why they are separate tabs.

What it can’t tell you

A change that holds still doesn’t forecast results. This was the intended product and it did not survive testing: a reliability-weighted composite of input changes adds no measurable R² to next month’s wOBA at 15-, 30- or 45-day resolution. A confirmed move tells you something real happened to his swing. It does not tell you he is about to hit.

It cannot tell you why. An injury, a mechanical cue, a new approach and a park change all look identical here.

How the expectation is built The model behind the carry rate, how to read R² against its ceiling, and what failed on the way there.

The model. Next window’s wOBA is regressed on four things: the player’s wOBA and xwOBA in the current window, and his wOBA and xwOBA over every strictly prior block of the same season. Fit on 2023–2025 and scored on 2026, which has since been looked at too many times to count as a clean test. The baseline never contains the future, which is the difference between a forecast and a description.

Read R² against the ceiling, never on its own. The target is itself a noisy measurement, so even a perfect forecast of true talent scores badly. R²_max = var(T)/var(T+e) with var(e) = E[σ²/n] and σ² = 0.2258 per plate appearance. The numbers appear in the tiles above.

What doesn’t help. The obvious refinement is “streaks the body corroborates will carry; streaks it does not will not.” A reliability-weighted composite of input changes adds a coefficient worth 0.004 of wOBA per standard deviation and no measurable R² at 15-, 30- or 45-day resolution. Splitting hot streaks by corroboration moves the carry rate 27% → 32%, CI [−12%, +22%], P(gap>0) = 0.73, so the forecast does not use it. The paradox worth sitting with: those input changes do persist (r ≈ .5) and still do not predict next month’s wOBA, because next month’s wOBA is barely predictable by anything.

What this answers How much of a hot or cold streak is likely to still be there next month, for every qualified player at once.

How to read it

carry is a discount rate, not a prediction. A hitter 240 points above his own baseline with 32% carry is still expected to be better than that baseline going forward — by roughly 77 points, not 240. Nobody is being told to regress to league average.

Where the trades are. Sell candidates are a large positive gap with low carry: the market is pricing a stretch the model does not believe. Buy candidates are the mirror — a large negative gap with low carry is a cold streak that is mostly not real, and those players are cheap for a reason that will not persist. Two rows with the same gap can have very different carry, and the difference is almost entirely whether the expected-stats line moved with the results or stayed put.

Read the R² tiles honestly. The model captures about 12% of the forecastable variance that exists at this horizon, which sounds bad until you see the ceiling tile next to it: next month’s wOBA is mostly noise, so a perfect forecaster scores 0.285, not 1.0. The tile on the right shows what last month’s results alone are worth — close to nothing, which is the reason this page exists.

What it can’t tell you

Over two weeks it’s starker. Across 574 scoreable two-week hot streaks in 2023–2025, only 19% of the gain was still there over the following two months, and streaks backed by better contact (a higher xwOBA) kept no more than lucky ones — 20% against 17%. What changes the answer is length, not contact: 30-day streaks kept 33% over the same two months and 45-day streaks 46%. See Streak history study, where every figure is judged over one fixed horizon.

How this differs from Real changes. carry is a forecast about results, fit across every player. Real changes’ held is an after-the-fact check on whether one player’s swing or approach change actually stuck. Use Real changes to find out whether something changed, and this tab to decide how much of the results to believe.

It cannot see injuries, or anything else off the field. The model knows results and expected stats. A player who is hurt, or about to be benched, looks like a player in a slump.

Streak history study on wOBA

“He’s earned it” mostly doesn’t last

Applies to this analysis

Case studies

What this answers Whether hot streaks that look “earned” — the ones where a hitter really is squaring the ball up — actually last longer than lucky ones.

How to read it

What counts as a streak. A hitter’s wOBA over a window of two weeks, 30 days or 45 days (at least 35 plate appearances per two weeks), compared with his own baseline and never with the league.

Two baselines. Career so far compares a window with every plate appearance he has had since 2023 before it (at least 300), and keeps only windows that are hot or cold by more than twice what sampling noise would produce (|z| ≥ 2, counting the noise in both the window and the baseline). Earlier-season rate is the original study: any window 80+ points above his rate earlier that same season. The data starts in 2023, so “career” means at most three prior seasons, and the career view’s headline years are 2024–2025.

Earned vs. lucky. A streak is earned when his xwOBA — the value of his contact, ignoring where the ball landed — moved at least 50 points with it (up for a hot streak, down for a slump), and lucky when it moved less than 20. Streaks in between are counted in “all” but in neither group.

Kept is the share of the gap (or the slump) still there over the following window of the same length, weighted by playing time. Back to baseline is the share whose next window was back at or past his baseline. Streaks with no qualified next window (injured, benched, sent down) are left out of both, which flatters the numbers slightly.

What it can’t tell you

Descriptive only. No model is fit here. 2026 is shown separately rather than pooled, because it has been looked at too many times to count as a clean test (why).

Hitters only, and it is history rather than advice. No pitcher version has been run, and nothing on this tab names a player who is hot right now — for that, use Streak carry forecast or Hot & cold right now.
