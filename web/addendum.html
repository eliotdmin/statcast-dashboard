Method

How to read every number on this site, in plain terms.

Baseball statistics mix two things: what a player is genuinely good at, and luck. Over a few weeks the luck is usually bigger. This site separates the two, and this page explains how — first the ideas that apply everywhere, then the caveats that belong to each analysis.

Three terms used throughout. A plate appearance is one turn at bat. wOBA is a single number for how much a hitter contributes per plate appearance, counting a home run for more than a single and a walk for less. Bat speed is how fast the bat is moving when it meets the ball — a measure of the swing itself, not of what happened to the ball.

What “% real” means

“% real” says how much of a stat is skill across all players — not how accurate one player’s number is.

Rank every hitter by some stat over six weeks, then imagine replaying those same six weeks. Some of the ranking would come back the same, because some players really are better. The rest would scramble, because it was luck. “% real” is the share that would come back. It is a property of the stat at that sample size, and every player gets the same figure.

The site uses it to correct every percentile: a stat that is only half real has its percentile pulled halfway back toward average. The solid bar is the corrected figure; where correcting moved it five points or more, a hatched extension shows what the uncorrected number said.

Example. Over six weeks in 2026, Alex Bregman’s wOBA was 98th percentile. wOBA is about half real at that sample, so it corrects to the 74th. His bat speed is 98% real at the same sample and barely moves.

The maths

For a stat over n plate appearances, ρ = var(true skill) / var(observed). It is measured by splitting each season into odd and even half-months and correlating the halves, corrected for the split with Spearman–Brown, ρ₀ = 2r/(1+r), and projected to other sample sizes by the same formula. The corrected percentile is 50 + ρ(raw − 50); that the correction coefficient equals ρ follows from the regression of true skill on the observed value.

How precisely it is known

We know almost exactly how trustworthy swing speed is; we know how trustworthy batting results are only roughly.

“% real” is itself measured from a few thousand player-seasons, so it has its own margin of error. For the swing measurements it is tiny. For the results measurements — the ones this site tells you to discount — it is wide. So the correction applied to a wOBA percentile is itself approximate, and it is approximate exactly where the correction matters most.

Example. Bat speed is .974 real, with a 95% range of .971 to .978. wOBA is .479, with a range of .418 to .545 — so “about half real” really means somewhere between about 42% and 55%.

The maths

Each ρ is bootstrapped by resampling player-seasons (1,000 draws). The interval appears on every % real chip and in the reliability table. It captures uncertainty in which players were measured, not in how their seasons were split, and projecting ρ to another sample size carries it along without widening it.

Real vs. big

A change can be real and still too small to matter, so every change is judged on both.

Two separate questions sit behind “did he change?”. Is the change more than luck would produce — and is it large enough to care about? A hitter can add half a mile an hour of bat speed that is certainly real and makes no difference. Another can look transformed over a fortnight on a change that is probably noise. The site answers both, separately, rather than folding them into one score.

The maths

Whether a change is real is its z: the change divided by the error two windows of that size would produce on their own. How big it is is expressed in standard deviations of how much players genuinely change over a season, so a “large” change means large relative to real movement, not relative to noise.

How much can be predicted at all

Next month’s results are mostly luck, so even a perfect forecaster would be wrong most of the time.

A forecast is usually graded out of 100%, as if a perfect one were possible. It is not. Over a month, a hitter bats about 90 times, and the outcome of each turn is close to a coin flip weighted by his skill. Even someone who knew every player’s true ability exactly would explain only about a quarter of how next month turns out. So every forecast here is graded against that ceiling, not against perfection — and the best models reach about a fifth of it.

The maths

Decomposing month-to-month differences for the same hitter (16,228 pairs) gives a per-plate-appearance variance of 0.2258, month-to-month drift in true skill of .038, and a spread of true skill across hitters of .035. At a typical month the realistic ceiling on R² is 0.264.

One season to treat with care

We have looked at 2026 too often to treat it as a fair test, so its results are shown but not trusted.

The honest way to test a forecast is to build it on some seasons and score it once on a season it has never seen. 2026 started as that test season. But it has since been checked, and re-checked, many times — and every look is a small chance to shape the answer to fit. So wherever 2026 appears it is shown separately and greyed out, and nothing on the site depends on it. The clean test is 2027, and the predictions for it are written down in advance.

By analysis

What each part of the site can and can’t tell you.

Luck-adjusted percentiles

A player’s rank over any stretch is corrected for how much of it is luck.

Pick a player and a date range and the site ranks him against everyone who played a similar amount over the same dates. Every rank is corrected as described above. It describes games already played: it does not say whether he will keep it up.

Swing & approach changes

A change in how a player swings shows up weeks before it shows up in his results.

Swing and approach measurements become reliable within a couple of weeks, far faster than results do. So a genuine mechanical change is visible here early. Each flagged change is also checked afterwards, against the following weeks, to see whether it stuck — about half do.

What it can’t tell you: that a real change will improve his results. This was tested directly and it did not hold: a change that sticks is real, but on this evidence it does not forecast the next month.

Hot and cold streaks

Most of a hot streak is gone within two months, and how long it has lasted says more than how good the contact looked.

A two-week hot streak keeps about a fifth of its gain over the following two months. A six-week streak keeps nearly half. Whether the hitter was “really squaring the ball up” during the streak makes no measurable difference. Slumps last longer than hot streaks, perhaps because some of them are injuries.

What it can’t tell you: why a streak happened. Streaks that come too late in the season to have two full months after them are left out, and those tend to fade faster, so the figures run slightly high for a streak happening in September.

The maths

Every streak is judged over one fixed horizon — the next 60 days — whatever its own length. Judging a two-week streak against the following two weeks compared one noisy sample with another; averaged over many streaks that barely changes the headline figure, but it made individual verdicts mostly noise.

Head-to-head odds

Predicting a player’s exact numbers barely beats guessing, so we only predict which of two players does better.

Forecasting a hitter’s exact line a month out is barely better than guessing league average. Picking which of two players does better is an easier question, so that is the one asked. The answer is a probability, and it is usually closer to a coin flip than people expect: over a month, two good regulars are often 55–45.

Each probability comes with a scoring record: every past matchup the same method was asked, and how often its favourite actually won. That record is also compared with the simplest rule available — back whoever was better last season — because beating a coin flip is too low a bar.

What it can’t tell you: for hitters over two weeks, anything more than a coin flip would — at that horizon it has no demonstrable skill. From a month out it beats the simple rule, but that rule alone already gets most of the way. It also cannot know about an injury or a demotion, which is why playing time is left for you to set.

Example. Aaron Judge against Alex Bregman — nearly the widest skill gap in baseball — comes out about 80% over two weeks, and still about 80% over the rest of a season.

The maths

Each player’s skill is his prior-season level, pulled toward the league average by about 330 plate appearances’ worth, then updated by this season. The difference between two players then carries three uncertainties — not knowing their skill, skill changing over the window, and luck over the plate appearances ahead — and the probability is Φ(Δ/√total). Accuracy is the Brier score, where always saying 50% scores .2500.

Sample size needed

Some numbers mean something within days; others need more than a season.

This is a reliability and power analysis. Ask it before reacting to a stat: it says how many plate appearances it takes before a ranking on that stat is mostly skill, and how many it takes to confirm a change you already suspect.

What it can’t tell you: anything specific to one player. It assumes an ordinary one; for a genuine outlier the true answer is smaller.

Example. Bat speed is 70% skill after 13 plate appearances. wOBA takes 525 — most of a season — and needs more than three seasons before a ranking on it is 90% skill. A pitcher who has lost a mile an hour off his fastball can be confirmed within 26 batters.
