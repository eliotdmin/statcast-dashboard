Statcast Reality Check · Technical report

Skill, Luck and Forecasting in Statcast Data, 2023–2026

The Statcast Reality Check project · Version 1 · 21 September 2026

Abstract

We ask whether pitch-level Statcast measurements say something about major-league hitters and pitchers that their outcome statistics do not, using 2,843,142 pitches from the 2023–2026 regular seasons. Split-half reliability and a variance decomposition show a sharp divide: what a hitter does — bat speed, swing length, whether he chases — is largely signal within a season, while outcomes that depend on the ball in play are largely noise, and sweet-spot rate shows no detectable year-over-year signal at all. At a one-month horizon a hitter's recent wOBA forecasts the next month worse than league average (R² −0.004), expected wOBA does better (0.015), and a 48-feature model reaches 0.057 against a derived noise ceiling of 0.264. None of eight model families beats a tuned Marcel baseline (0.0584 against 0.0565), whose advantage comes from regressing harder rather than from any feature. A slump measured against a hitter's own norm carries essentially no information about the next month, while the skills beneath it persist. Hot streaks keep about a seventh of their gain over the following two weeks and about two-fifths of a six-week streak's gain over the next six weeks, with no advantage for streaks backed by contact quality. A head-to-head estimator that orders two players over a coming window is well calibrated at thirty days but over- and under-confident at shorter and longer horizons, and most of its skill is already available from a naive rule on last season's wOBA. Secondary analyses cover the wOBA–xwOBA gap, the count, platoon splits and park-level differences in bat tracking; tertiary analyses cover pitching orthodoxy, the 2026 strike zone and two-strike swing adjustments. The 2026 test season was queried repeatedly, so its figures should be read as optimistic by an unknown amount.

Keywords: Statcast; split-half reliability; shrinkage estimation; forecasting; Marcel; expected wOBA; baseball.

1Introduction

The animating question was simple and, it turned out, harder than it looked: can this data tell you something about a hitter or pitcher that his statistics alone do not? Baseball has an obvious version of this problem. A hitter goes 2-for-30 over ten days. Is he hurt, is he doing something different with his swing, or has he simply been unlucky? The traditional answer was to wait and see. Statcast offers the possibility of an answer now, because it measures what the hitter did rather than only what happened afterwards.

Everything below is an attempt to establish whether that possibility is real. The short answer, stated up front so that nothing here reads as a slow reveal, is: much less than we expected, and in a narrower set of circumstances than we expected.

The analyses are reported in three tiers. Primary analyses (§4) are the ones the Statcast Reality Check dashboard is built on: which measurements carry signal, how much of a recent stretch persists, how well any of it forecasts, and how confidently two players can be ordered. Secondary analyses (§5) qualify or extend those results without being used directly by the dashboard. Tertiary analyses (§6) are exploratory questions outside the dashboard's scope, mostly nulls, reported so that nothing tested is left out.

Written for someone who knows baseball but has never seen this repository. No statistical background is assumed; the methods are explained where they are first used. Nothing here requires reading anything else first.

2Data

Since 2015, every pitch thrown in a Major League Baseball game has been tracked by a camera and radar system called Statcast. It records how fast the pitch left the hand, where it crossed the plate, how much it moved, and — if the batter swings — how fast the bat was moving, how hard the ball was struck, at what angle, and where it landed. Since 2024 it also records the geometry of the swing itself: the upward tilt of the bat at contact, its horizontal direction, and the point in space where bat met ball.

This project holds four complete regular seasons of that data — 2023 through 2026 — in a single local database. That is 2,843,142 pitches across 119 columns, of which roughly 486,000 are balls put in play and 1.33 million are tracked swings. The database is 1.8 gigabytes and lives on one laptop.

Each half-month block also carries surface counting statistics — games, at-bats, hits by type, home runs, walks, hit-by-pitch and sacrifices — with the player's team and most-played fielding position, checked against published season lines. Aaron Judge's 2024 line matches on every field except strikeouts (170 against 171); Tarik Skubal's 2024 innings come to 190.2 against an official 192.0.

Data caveats

  • wOBA is computed, not read from the stored column. The pitch-level woba_value field credits a batter who reaches on an error (0.900), a fielder's choice (0.900) or a dropped third strike (0.700), so it measures reaching base rather than wOBA and runs about nine points high. wOBA here credits only singles, doubles, triples, home runs, walks and hit-by-pitch, and matches Savant's published figures to within .003 in every season. Two sets of figures predate that computation: the absolute values in the count table (Table 11), which run 6–9 points high, and the forecasting R² comparisons of §§4.5–4.7. Both are near-invariant to a near-uniform offset, so the relative structure and the comparisons stand; neither has been re-run.
  • Strikeouts omit strikeout double plays, typically zero to two per player-season. They are left out so that K% and reliability figures stay comparable across the archive.
  • Innings pitched run a few outs short, because outs made on the bases are not attributed to a batter.
  • sz_top changed meaning in 2026 (§6.5), so every strike-zone measurement here is in absolute height.
  • Bat-tracking readings differ slightly by ballpark (§5.4).

3Methods

Five ideas do almost all of the work in this report. They are worth understanding before the results, because in several cases the method is the finding.

3.1Reliability: does a statistic repeat itself?

Suppose you want to know whether "ground-ball rate" is a real property of a hitter or mostly an accident of which balls happened to be hit on the ground. Split his season into two halves that ought to be equivalent — we used odd-numbered calendar months against even-numbered ones — compute the statistic in each half, and correlate the two halves across all hitters.

If the correlation is near 1, the statistic repeats: hitters who ground out a lot in April also do so in May, and the differences between them are real. If it is near 0, the two halves are unrelated and the entire apparent spread between players was noise. Because each half contains only half the data, this understates the reliability of the full season, and a correction called Spearman–Brown [1, 2] restores it.

This single number decides whether a statistic can support a claim about an individual player. A statistic with reliability 0.16 cannot, no matter how large the difference between two players looks.

3.2Signal share: how much of a change is real?

Every measured quantity is the truth plus an error, and variances add: var(observed) = var(true) + var(noise). The noise part can be computed from sample sizes alone — for a rate it is p(1−p)/n — so the true spread can be recovered by subtraction without ever observing a true value.

Applied to changes rather than levels, this answers the question every "breakout" article dodges: if a hitter's ground-ball rate fell ten points, how much of that was real? We computed the answer for every metric in the dataset, and the ordering turned out to be the single most useful thing the project produced.

3.3Shrinkage: why you should believe less than you see

If a metric is only 30% signal, then an observed change of ten points should be reported as three. Formally the estimate is pulled toward the league average by a factor var(true) / (var(true) + standard error²).

This is not a conservatism heuristic. Charles Stein proved in 1961 [3] that for three or more quantities, the obvious estimator — just use what you measured — is always beaten by one that shrinks toward a common centre. It was so counterintuitive that it is still called Stein's paradox. In 1977 Efron and Morris [4] illustrated it using the batting averages of eighteen players after forty-five at-bats, shrinking them toward the league mean to predict the rest of the season; the shrunk estimates cut total squared error by roughly a factor of 3.5. As §4.6 records, the central empirical finding of this project turned out to be a rediscovery of that result.

3.4The noise ceiling: what is the best a forecaster could do?

Forecast accuracy is usually reported as R², the fraction of variation explained, where 1.0 is perfection. But if the thing you are predicting is mostly coin flips, perfection is unreachable and R² near 1 is not the right benchmark.

We derived the real ceiling. For two months of the same hitter in the same season, E[(w₁−w₂)²] = σ²(1/pa₁ + 1/pa₂) + var(drift). Regressing 16,228 squared differences on the sample-size term identifies both pieces: the per-plate-appearance outcome variance is 0.2258, real month-to-month movement in skill has a standard deviation of .038, and true talent across 670 hitters has a spread of just .035.

At a typical month of 87 plate appearances, that means a forecaster who knew every hitter's true talent exactly would still only score R² = 0.264, because the target is mostly noise. Every forecasting number in §4.5 should be read against 0.264, not against 1.0. This changes the interpretation of every result completely: a model scoring 0.057 is not failing, it is capturing a fifth of everything available.

3.5Holdout testing and pre-registration

Any model can be made to fit data it has already seen. Every forecasting result here was fit on the 2023–2025 seasons and scored against 2026. 2026 is no longer a clean holdout, though. It has since been queried dozens of times across this project — four model specifications at four window lengths, two player types, several plate-appearance floors, and a streak subgroup analysis — and each of those looks is a degree of freedom spent. The honest name for what 2026 became partway through is a development set, and every 2026 figure below should be read as optimistic by an unknown amount. Uncertainty is reported as bootstrap intervals: resample the test rows three thousand times with replacement, recompute the whole statistic each time, and report the middle 95% — which captures sampling noise, not this optimism.

Where a hypothesis had a predicted direction, that direction was written down before the query ran. Five predictions were pre-registered in this way. Three of them failed, and they are reported at the same length as the successes.

Going forward, PREREGISTRATION.md freezes five new predictions against 2027, the next genuinely clean evaluation, before that season's data exists. A further three, sealed on 20 September 2026, ask whether a late-season decline among hitters aged 32 and over carries into the next season beyond Marcel's age adjustment; they may not be run until six season transitions of block-level data exist.

Several later analyses (§§4.2, 4.4, 4.8) fit no coefficients at all: they evaluate a fixed rule or describe what followed a streak. Repeated looks at 2026 bias them less, and 2026 is reported separately where it matters.

4Primary analyses: skill, luck and forecasting

These analyses underpin every estimate on the dashboard: the “% real” figure on each metric, the verdict on a recent stretch, the streak forecast, the head-to-head odds, and the decision to shrink toward a baseline rather than to trust a model.

4.1Which measurements are signal: the calibration table holds

Applying the signal-share method of §3.2 to every metric, across 222 hitters with 250+ plate appearances in both 2025 and 2026, produced an ordering that reframed everything after it:

The ordering is the lesson. Everything a hitter does — how fast and how long he swings, whether he swings — is measurable in a single season. Everything that depends on what happened to the ball afterwards is mostly luck at one season of sample. Sweet-spot rate has literally no detectable year-over-year signal across the league: the entire apparent spread is sampling noise. Any story about a hitter changing his launch angle profile, built on one season of sweet-spot rate, is a story about nothing.

On the pitching side the split is sharper still. Arm angle, release extension, fastball velocity and spin rate are all 97–100% signal, while walk rate and edge rate are 1%. A pitcher's body is precisely measured; his command is not.

Caveat. These are shares of a season-to-season change. The reliability of a level is not the reliability of a change, and shorter windows hold much less: a monthly change in bat speed is 34% signal against 85% over a season.

4.2How precisely reliability is known holds

Every shrunk percentile on the dashboard inherits a reliability estimate, because the shrinkage coefficient is the reliability. Those estimates are correlations over a few hundred player-seasons and have sampling error of their own. We recomputed the split-half reliabilities from the published half-month data, reproducing the pipeline's values to within 0.0009, and bootstrapped them over 1,572 qualifying player-seasons.

The bootstrap resamples player-seasons, so it captures which players were measured but not how each season was split into halves, and Spearman–Brown projection to other sample sizes carries the uncertainty without widening it.

4.3Persistence: skills versus results

This analysis addresses the question the project began with. A hitter is slumping. What, if anything, does that tell you about next month?

Skills are stable. Results are not. And the gap between the two is enormous: bat speed repeats at .919 over a single month while wOBA repeats at .135.

The sharper version removes each hitter's own career mean, which is what separates the slump from the hitter. Once that is done, the carryover collapses to nothing: wOBA −.038, −.080, −.055 across the three window lengths, and expected wOBA +.004, −.000, +.072.

Caveat, recorded because it matters. Demeaning a short panel induces a mechanical negative bias of roughly −1/(T−1). The small negatives above are therefore consistent with zero carryover rather than genuine anti-persistence, and should be read that way.

The operational conclusion

A slump, measured as a deviation in results from a hitter's own norm, predicts essentially nothing about the following month. But the underlying skills — whiff rate, bat speed, exit velocity — persist strongly. So the correct response to a slumping hitter is not to ask how bad the slump is, but to ask whether anything he does has changed. If his bat speed and whiff rate are where they were, the slump carries no information. If they have moved, that movement is real and will persist.

Caveat. Persisting is not the same as leading. A change in chase rate is detectable within a month, but this month's change predicts next month's change in production at +0.046 ± 0.036 — nothing — while its same-month association is −0.370 ± 0.041. Chase rate moves with results rather than ahead of them.

4.4How much of a hot or cold streak lasts

§4.3 shows that a deviation in results from a hitter's own norm carries little about the next month. The dashboard's streak analyses ask the same question in the form a reader meets it: a hitter has been hot or cold over a named stretch, so how much of the gap survives? These analyses are descriptive and fit no coefficients. Hitters only.

4.4.1Length makes a streak believable; contact quality does not holds

A hot streak is a window whose wOBA is at least .080 above the hitter's own strictly earlier rate that season, with at least 35 PA in the window and 100 in the baseline. It is earned if expected wOBA rose at least .050 over the same baseline, and lucky if it rose less than .020. The share kept is the PA-weighted gain over the next window of equal length, divided by the gain in the streak. Intervals resample players.

Persistence roughly triples from two weeks to six, and 2026 reproduces the ladder almost exactly. A rise in expected wOBA alongside the streak adds nothing at any length. At about 50 plate appearances expected wOBA is itself mostly noise, and its own jump carried only 8–9% into the next two weeks. This agrees with the variance decomposition of §3.4, under which at most about a fifth of a two-week gap against a ~200 PA baseline can be real.

Contact quality measured as hard-hit rate (up at least eight points, against down) favoured contact-backed streaks in sign at all three lengths in 2023–2025: 17% against 10%, 34% against 21%, and 49% against 38%. The nine most convincing contact-backed two-week streaks of 2023–2025 (the three largest expected-wOBA jumps in each season) illustrate the ladder. Of the eight with a scoreable next window, one kept at least half its gain (Aaron Judge, May 2024). Several others ran modestly above baseline for the rest of the season. Nine cases illustrate the ladder; they are not evidence for it.

Clearing a significance bar does not make a hot streak durable, and slumps persist more than hot streaks at every length. Caveats. This is not a like-for-like comparison with §4.4.1. It uses a different baseline, a filter that selects the most extreme windows, and different seasons, so “3% against 14%” is not a clean effect of the baseline. The hot–cold asymmetry is compatible with injuries and ageing — real declines that look like slumps — but injured-list data is not in the archive, so nothing here identifies them. “Career” means at most three prior seasons. Between 78% and 92% of significant streaks are earned, which leaves the lucky groups too small to compare.

4.4.3Judging a streak on a longer follow-up holds

Scoring a two-week streak against the following two weeks compares one noisy sample with another. Fixing the follow-up at sixty days separates two questions.

The aggregate share kept barely moves, because follow-up noise is mean-zero and independent of how the streak was selected, so over a hundred or more streaks it cancels. On the career view, hot streaks kept 3%→0%, 3%→8% and 14%→12% at 15, 30 and 45 days, and cold streaks 9%→11%, 20%→20% and 24%→24%.

Individual verdicts move a great deal at short lengths. The share of two-week streaks called “stuck” (still at least half the gap) falls from 20% to 8% for hot streaks and from 21% to 7% for slumps, so about two-thirds of those calls reflect luck in the follow-up window. At 30 and 45 days little changes, because the original follow-up was already long.

Caveat. A sixty-day horizon cannot score late-season streaks: 171 of 766 career-view streaks are too late. On the season view, holding the streaks fixed shows that nearly all of the apparent rise under the longer horizon (14% to 19% at 15 days) comes from dropping those streaks, not from the horizon. Late-season streaks keep less. The figures therefore describe early- and mid-season streaks, and probably run a little high for a streak happening now.

4.5Forecasting the next window

The largest single effort went into one question: given what a hitter did in one stretch, how well can you predict what he does in the next? This is the practical question behind every roster decision, and it is the question on which expected statistics were supposed to deliver.

Two quantities matter throughout. wOBA (weighted on-base average) assigns each plate-appearance outcome its average run value, scaled to look like on-base percentage; it is a description of what happened. Expected wOBA replaces each batted ball's actual outcome with the league-average result of balls hit at the same exit velocity and launch angle, leaving strikeouts and walks alone; it is a description of how the ball was struck, deliberately blind to where it was hit, to the ballpark and to the defence.

At one month, using what a hitter just hit to predict what he hits next is worse than predicting league average for everybody. Expected wOBA is better at every horizon.

Two things we predicted in advance turned out wrong. We expected expected-wOBA's advantage to shrink as the window lengthened, since wOBA's own noise falls with sample. Instead it is humped: it rises from one month to two, holds through three, and nearly vanishes over a full season. We have a story for this but not a mechanism, and the three-month row has the smallest test sample. prediction failed

We also expected the optimal weight on expected wOBA to fall as sample grew. Instead, fitting both together within buckets of playing time, the coefficient on actual wOBA is negative in every bucket above 55 plate appearances. Conditional on how the ball was struck, what actually happened is not merely uninformative — it points the wrong way. A hitter who beat his expected output should be marked down. prediction failed

4.5.2A richer model helps, and one of its best features is embarrassing holds

Adding contact quality, plate discipline, batted-ball mix, context and swing geometry — 48 features in total — raises one-month accuracy from 0.015 to 0.057, or about a fifth of the theoretical ceiling. Swing geometry earns its place: adding attack angle, swing tilt, contact depth and bat speed produces the single largest jump after context, and in the simplified linear version bat speed carries the second-largest coefficient of any input, ahead of expected wOBA itself.

The uncomfortable control

Plate appearances turned up as the second most important feature, so we ran it alone. How often a manager writes a hitter into the lineup predicts next month better than how hard he hits the ball — 0.032 against expected wOBA's 0.015. That is not a hitting statistic. It is the manager's private information about health, matchups and what he has seen in person. It is retained in headline numbers because a real forecaster would observe it too, but it is now always reported alongside the version without it.

4.5.3The trade-off nobody states holds

A statistic can describe what happened, agree with its own next measurement, or forecast the future. These are different properties and they trade off almost perfectly:

4.6The Marcel baseline

All of the above was encouraging until we compared it to the correct benchmark.

Marcel — named after the monkey from Friends, and published by Tom Tango in 2004 [5] precisely so that people with complicated systems would have something to clear — does three things. It averages a hitter's last three seasons weighted 5/4/3. It regresses that average hard toward the league mean by adding a block of fictional league-average plate appearances. And it nudges the result for age. It has no batted-ball data, no tracking data, and no model. It is about five lines of arithmetic.

Before comparing, we made two adjustments against our own models, because the alternative is a strawman. Marcel's regression constant was tuned on training years, exactly as our models' hyperparameters were. And every model was re-run with the same career history Marcel sees, since we had handicapped ours to a single window.

Result: a draw, and then a loss

At a one-month horizon, tuned Marcel scores 0.0584 and our 48-feature gradient-boosted model scores 0.0565. The difference is −0.002 [−0.039, +0.033]; the probability our model is better is 0.46. Giving the models Marcel's history does not change it. Eight model families were eventually tried — ridge with a cross-validated penalty of 56,000, elastic net, partial least squares, principal components, and three variants that use Marcel as an offset and model only its residual. Not one of them beats it.

The Marcel-as-offset result is the most informative. If tracking data held information Marcel lacks, modelling Marcel's residual is exactly where it would appear. It does not appear.

On Marcel's home ground — forecasting a full season from prior seasons — it is worse than a draw. Tuned Marcel scores 0.164. A twenty-feature model built on past Statcast scores −0.060, meaning it is worse than predicting league average for everyone. With 267 training seasons and twenty correlated predictors, that is a textbook variance failure. Meanwhile a plain shrunk average of past expected wOBA ties Marcel exactly, 0.166 to 0.164.

The value is in the shrinking, not in the features.

We then tried to build a better Marcel, testing four evidence-backed improvements in sequence. Only one worked, and it was not a feature. Changing the regression constant from the classic 1200 to a tuned 3000 moved season accuracy from 0.0745 to 0.1640 — a bigger gain than every Statcast feature in this project combined. The reason is stark: a hitter's weighted three-year record correlates with his next season at only 0.40, so roughly 60% of his apparent deviation from league average should be discarded even with two thousand plate appearances of history. Classic Marcel keeps 87%.

4.7Where measurement does win tentative

One segment survives. Marcel's blind spot is a player it has never seen: with no history it can only predict league average. Splitting the test set by how much prior playing time a hitter has, the model beats Marcel by +0.049 in the bottom quartile and loses by 0.040 in the top, monotonically across all four. By age, the only segment whose interval excludes zero is 22 to 25 years old, where the model wins by +0.065.

A caveat on the natural summary: expected wOBA alone loses to Marcel in every age bucket, including the youngest. What beats Marcel is the full measurement model, not the expected statistic. And this was a post-hoc split across eight segments, where one interval excluding zero is roughly what chance produces. It is a hypothesis for next season, not a finding.

We also predicted the advantage would concentrate among hitters whose measurements had just moved — Marcel literally cannot see a three-mph bat-speed gain. Among the top decile of movers the gap was −0.001, probability 0.50. Exactly nothing. The blind spot we were most confident about does not matter at this horizon. prediction failed

4.8Head to head: ordering two players over a coming window

§§4.5–4.6 establish that a point forecast of next month's wOBA explains little. “Who will be better over the next N weeks” is a different question, because only the sign of the gap has to clear the noise.

The estimator starts from each player's prior-season level, weighted 1/.8/.6 across seasons and regressed toward the league by about 330 PA. It combines that with his current season by precision and gives P(A ahead) = Φ(Δ/√V). V sums three independent variances: uncertainty in each estimate, true-talent drift (sd .0381 per month, treated as a random walk), and sampling (σ² = .2258 per PA). It was backtested at every half-month boundary of 2023–2026, with estimates from strictly prior blocks and players paired disjointly at each boundary. No coefficients were fitted.

The estimator is well calibrated. Estimates of .52, .57, .62, .67 and .74 were followed by win rates of .520, .558, .632, .677 and .739; only the top bucket drifts (.849 against .767, n = 60). Pitchers (2,774 pairs) track similarly.

Three results are worth keeping. First, ranking a pair on raw season wOBA is worse than a coin flip (.2646 against .2500). Its reliability term is five times the shrunk estimator's, so it is overconfident rather than short of information — the result of §4.5.1 in an independent form. Second, the estimator is honest but rarely far from 50%: the median matchup lands .076 from a coin flip for hitters and .066 for pitchers. Third, sampling is 63% of the uncertainty, drift 32% and true talent under 5%. Sampling noise falls as 1/n while drift accumulates as √t, so a longer window does not make a matchup more decisive.

The cost of being allowed to look repeatedly was measured on the same data. A variance-adaptive confidence sequence, valid at every sample size, separated the estimator from each alternative wherever a fixed-horizon 95% interval did, at 1.6 times the width.

4.8.3Against a naive prior: real skill, mostly borrowed holds

The comparators above convert a raw gap into a probability through the estimator's own variance model, so none of them is naive. The naive baseline slices matchups by a single raw gap and predicts the win rate that slice actually had. It is folded by symmetry, uses ten quantile slices with thin slices shrunk toward the overall rate, and is fitted leave-one-season-out, so no season is scored by rates learned from itself. Differences are bootstrapped by resampling block boundaries.

Over two weeks the hitter estimator has no demonstrable skill. Against a coin flip it gains +.0022, 95% CI [−.0018, +.0060], so a lopsided two-week probability is a lean, not a forecast. From a month out the skill is real but mostly borrowed. Ranking on last season's wOBA and looking up the historical win rate captures 64–82% of it; combining prior form with the current season adds the rest, significantly but modestly. For pitchers the estimator beats every naive rule at 30, 60 and 90 days. Re-slicing the estimator's own gap empirically scores no better than its Φ probabilities at 30–90 days. So the variance model converts a ranking into well-calibrated probabilities, and the ranking is the whole game. Resolution is about .018 at every horizon, which caps what any better ranking could add.

Against Marcel, the estimator's hitter skill is not demonstrable. Scoring the same pairs against three more naive priors built from official MLB season lines (last season, career before this season, and a Marcel projection: 5/4/3 weights, regressed with 3,000 PA and age-adjusted) gives a different picture for hitters. On a data refresh that moves the estimator's own figures slightly (30 days: .2396), a slice on the Marcel gap ties the estimator at every horizon (.2464 against .2485 at 15 days, .2420 against .2396 at 30, .2340 against .2345 at 60, and .2304 against .2320 at 90), with every interval spanning zero. Last season alone and career alone lose to the estimator from 30 days on. For pitchers the estimator beats Marcel at every horizon, with every interval excluding zero. Marcel draws on official lines back to 2020, which the estimator's own prior does not, so part of its showing may be longer history rather than method.

Caveats. Pairs are disjoint at each boundary but overlap across boundaries and reuse the same players all season, so the effective sample is smaller than the count. Playing-time floors select for regulars: injuries, demotions and benchings leave the sample rather than being scored. Marcel has not been run as a pairwise forecaster.

5Secondary analyses

Results that qualify the primary analyses — an unexplained persistence in the wOBA–xwOBA gap, the size of the count effect, the reliability of platoon splits, a measurement artefact in bat tracking that bears directly on how small a bat-speed change can be trusted, and a check on the constant per-PA variance behind every interval.

5.1The gap between wOBA and expected wOBA

When a hitter's actual wOBA exceeds his expected wOBA, the conventional reading is luck.

Within a season the gap behaves like noise. Its split-half reliability is 0.164, near the bottom of everything we measured — against 0.978 for bat speed, 0.879 for exit velocity and 0.448 for wOBA itself. A statistic that repeats with itself that weakly cannot support a claim about any individual hitter.

Across seasons, however, it persists. The same gap correlates with itself at +0.267, +0.302 and +0.238 over three independent year-pairs. Those two facts were in tension, and the tension was the interesting part.

By batted-ball type, the only systematic bias is on popups. Expected wOBA over-rates popups by 17.6 points per batted ball, though popups are only 7% of batted balls. Ground balls show no gap (+0.0008), line drives and fly balls only small ones (+0.0043 and −0.0046), and there is no league-wide offset by count. So the gap persists for hitters, and no mechanism tested so far explains it.

Caveat. These figures depend on computing wOBA from events rather than from the stored woba_value column (§2). Measured with that column, ground balls appear to beat their expected value by 23 points per batted ball, because it credits a batter who reaches on an error. That is a property of the column, not of the hitters.

One clean asymmetry did emerge and is worth keeping. Run identically on 1,335 pitcher-seasons, the gap does not persist: +0.104, −0.021, +0.161 across the same three year-pairs, and a split-half reliability of 0.213. Whatever drives the hitter version is a batter attribute. A pitcher does not choose his launch angles and does not run to first base.

On pitchers more generally, expected wOBA against beats actual wOBA against at predicting next season by 34% in relative terms — a much larger edge than the hitter version. And strikeout rate and walk rate alone reach 0.156 against expected wOBA's 0.170, using no batted-ball data whatsoever. That is the DIPS result from 1999 [6], reproduced with modern data: what a pitcher controls is mostly the outcomes that never become a batted ball.

On expected ERA, which is the natural next question: it is not in this archive, and it would add nothing. Savant's own glossary [7] defines it as "a simple 1:1 translation of xwOBA, converted to the ERA scale." It is a rescaling and carries no independent information. A structural decomposition of expected ERA against ERA would be the analysis above plus sequencing, bullpen inheritance, and the official scorer's earned-versus-unearned judgement, which this data does not contain at all.

5.2The count, and the largest effect we found

The question was posed plainly: if a plate appearance arrives at a given count, what does it eventually produce — counting everything that happens afterwards, not merely contact in that count. A plate appearance that reaches 3–0 has also passed through 2–0, 1–0 and 0–0, so the cells are nested by construction.

The range from the worst count to the best is .351 of wOBA. Across 286 hitters with a thousand or more plate appearances in these four seasons, the range from worst to best is .185. The count matters 1.89 times as much as the identity of the hitter, and on standard deviations rather than ranges the factor is four.

Put concretely: an average major-league hitter, at 3–0, out-produces the best hitter in baseball. The same hitter, at 0–2, is worse than the worst. The first strike is the most expensive single event in the sequence — 0–0 to 0–1 costs .048, and 0–1 to 0–2 costs a further .068.

This is the largest effect in the report and it was the last thing we looked at, which is why it is reported as secondary rather than built into the dashboard. It also reframes the pitching work in §§6.1–6.4: if the count dominates hitter identity by that margin, then the value of any individual pitch is mostly its effect on the count, which makes pitch selection a count-management problem rather than a deception problem. That reframing is untested.

5.3Platoon splits are a real individual trait holds

The average hitter is 31 points of wOBA better against opposite-handed pitching. The spread between hitters in how large their split is comes out at 0.0414, of which sampling noise accounts for 0.0217, leaving a true spread of 0.0353 — 72% signal.

So "he handles same-handed pitching unusually well" is a real, measurable property. But 28% of the apparent spread on any published platoon leaderboard is noise, which means the extremes are systematically overstated. A hitter shown with a 90-point split most likely has a true split nearer 70. This matters operationally, because platoon splits drive pinch-hitting and bullpen decisions in live games, and the operational version rarely shrinks.

5.4Bat speed is not measured identically in every ballpark holds

We compared each team's own hitters at home against the same hitters on the road. Same players, different sensor installations, so any systematic gap is the ballpark's tracking rather than the hitting. Home-minus-road bat speed varies across the thirty parks with a standard deviation of 0.23 mph, and home-minus-road exit velocity with a standard deviation of 0.46.

The tell is that the two are correlated at +0.456. Bat speed and exit velocity are separately derived measurements; a park where both read high, for visiting and home hitters alike, is a park whose installation runs hot. Against a true year-over-year bat-speed spread of 1.01 mph, a 0.23 mph measurement artifact is not negligible for the many changes in the 0.3–0.6 range that this project had been treating as marginal but real.

5.5Is the per-PA outcome variance constant? null

Every uncertainty on the dashboard uses one per-PA variance, σ² = .2258 (§3.4). Because wOBA weights outcomes from 0 to about 2, a hitter who reaches base more often, and for more bases, should have a larger per-PA second moment. If so, the constant understates uncertainty around the best hitters.

Hitters were stratified by prior-season rate, never by the blocks being measured. Sorting on the same season's wOBA would put lucky, high-variance seasons in the top stratum by construction. σ² was then recovered in each stratum as the slope of the adjacent-block regression of §3.4, from 8,657 pairs and 421 hitters. The strata are non-monotone and every interval overlaps; top minus bottom is +0.032, 95% CI [−0.066, +0.137]. No gradient is detectable, and the constant stands.

Caveats. The theoretical argument is sound, and the top of the interval would be a 60% effect for the best hitters; the data can only say it is not detectable. The adjacent-block estimator is also weakly identified from aggregated data. As the block PA floor rises from 10 to 40, σ² falls from .186 to .126 while the implied drift absorbs the difference. That is evidence that block aggregates cannot re-measure σ², not evidence against .2258. The direct test needs per-player second moments from the pitch-level data.

6Tertiary analyses: pitching, the strike zone and two-strike swings

Exploratory questions outside the dashboard's scope, reported for completeness. Most are nulls. Four pieces of pitching orthodoxy were tested. Three produced nothing, and the fourth was real but not for the reason usually given.

6.1Does mixing your pitches work? null

Every broadcast says a pitcher must "keep hitters honest" by varying what he throws. We measured the variety of a pitcher's arsenal using entropy — a pitcher who throws 40/30/20/10 has an effective arsenal of about 3.6 pitches, not four — and asked whether broader mixing predicted better results. It did not.

That null was unsatisfying enough that we re-ran it under the conditions most favourable to the hypothesis. If variety matters anywhere, it should matter the third time through the batting order, when a hitter has already seen you twice in the same game. Across 337 starter-seasons, the relationship between arsenal breadth and the third-time-through penalty was −0.0027 ± 0.0040 — an interval spanning zero in both directions against a league spread of 0.074.

A negative result that survives being deliberately re-run under favourable conditions is worth considerably more than the original. The practical reading: a fifth pitch is not what keeps a starter alive in the sixth inning.

6.2The third time through the order is real holds

The penalty itself is genuine and sizeable. Measured within pitcher — comparing each pitcher against his own average, so that "good pitchers are allowed to go deeper" cannot manufacture the effect — a plate appearance is worth .0094 below that pitcher's norm the first time through and .0146 above it the third. That is a swing of 24 points of wOBA from the first pass to the third.

6.3Tunnelling null

The theory is that two pitches released from the same point but finishing in different places are harder to distinguish, so the second should generate extra swings and misses. We examined every pair of consecutive different pitch types within a plate appearance, bucketed by the distance between release points, and compared the whiff rate against the baseline for that exact combination of previous pitch, current pitch, and count.

Across 340,000 swings the excess whiff rate wandered between −0.24 and +0.14 percentage points with no ordering at all. Nothing.

An important caveat we did not resolve. Release-point distance is a crude proxy. The quantity that actually matters is how far apart the two trajectories are at the hitter's commit point, roughly 23 feet from the plate, which is computable from the position, velocity and acceleration vectors in this data and was never computed. So what was shown is that release-point similarity does nothing; "tunnelling does not work" is a stronger claim than the evidence supports.

6.4Velocity decay, and a lesson worth more than the result null

Do starters who lose more velocity over a start get punished for it? The naive approach sorts 492 starts by how fast the fastball was falling and compares early to late results. It produces a beautiful, monotone, wrong answer: across five quintiles the correlation between decay and the late-game penalty was +0.905 — in the opposite direction to the hypothesis, apparently showing that pitchers who gained velocity collapsed hardest.

The fifth row explains it. That group's early wOBA was .2497, wildly better than everyone else's. Those are starts where a pitcher was dominating, was left in, and was still airing it out in the seventh. Their late-game "collapse" is regression from an unsustainable start.

Compared within pitcher — each of his starts against his own average, 8,250 starts from 307 pitchers — the effect is +0.0031 ± 0.0024, indistinguishable from zero and with the sign flipped. We keep this as the project's canonical example that monotonicity across five bins is not evidence of causation; it is what selection effects usually look like.

6.5The strike zone and the 2026 challenge system

In 2026 Major League Baseball introduced a challenge system allowing teams to appeal ball and strike calls to automated tracking. This is a large, recent, externally-imposed change to the game — close to a natural experiment — so we measured what it did to the shape of the called zone.

Called-strike rate on taken pitches is measured in bands of absolute height above the ground, which the rulebook fixes and no data provider can redefine, rather than relative to sz_top, the data's own field for the top of each batter's zone. holds

Why not relative to sz_top

sz_top had 321,120 distinct values across the league in 2024 and 353,643 in 2025, consistent with an operator estimating it pitch by pitch. In 2026 it had 390, exactly one per batter, and averaged 2.6 inches lower: MLB replaced the per-pitch estimate with a formula. Measured against it, the top of the 2026 zone appears to expand by 32.8 percentage points; in absolute height it shrank.

This also explains a number we had produced days earlier and not understood. The spread between the best and worst pitch-framing catchers collapsed from 15.5 runs to 10.6. A crisper zone leaves less to frame.

6.6Choking up is real, nearly universal, and appears to work holds

Bat tracking makes it possible to measure, for the first time at scale, whether hitters actually shorten their swing with two strikes. They do. Across 521 hitter-seasons, average bat speed with two strikes is 1.37 mph slower than without, and 89% of hitters do it.

The size of the adjustment varies a lot between hitters, and those who shorten up most strike out less, whiff less, and produce more in two-strike counts. All three correlations clear their intervals.

This is not causal and we did not make it so. Hitters with better bat control can afford to shorten up and are also better contact hitters anyway, so the adjustment may be a marker of a skill rather than its cause. The discriminating test — does the same hitter do better in the seasons he shortens up more — was specified and never run.

7Discussion

7.1Limitations

  • One test year, and it stopped being a holdout. Every forecasting number rests on 2026, which was queried dozens of times across model specifications and window lengths over the course of this project — so beyond the ordinary problem of resting on a single season, these figures should be read as optimistic by an unknown amount. Season-level results rest on 245 hitters in a single season. The bootstrap intervals reflect sampling within that year, not the year-to-year variability of the whole exercise, and they do not capture this optimism either. The next clean test is 2027, against predictions frozen in PREREGISTRATION.md before that data exists.
  • A four-season archive is a short runway. The season-level model had 267 training rows where professional systems train on twenty years. Its failure is most plausibly a sample-size failure rather than a verdict on the features, but that is an explanation and not a test.
  • Swing tracking cannot ever have a long history. It begins in 2024. Even backfilling to 2015 would give exit velocity eight more seasons and swing geometry none.
  • Marcel is the floor, not the standard. Steamer and ZiPS are substantially stronger and neither was tested; access to their pre-season files is gated. "No better than the baseline" is therefore a weaker statement than it sounds.
  • Survivorship in the panel. Requiring forty plate appearances in both the input and target window conditions on continued playing time, which is itself informative about performance.
  • Hyperparameters were fixed by judgement, not tuned, and model selection was not nested inside the bootstrap, so the intervals do not account for specification search.
  • Post-hoc segmentation. The age and history splits were run after the main result was known. Eight segments were examined; one interval excluding zero is approximately what chance produces.
  • Several causal questions were left as correlations — most notably whether shortening the swing with two strikes causes better two-strike results.
  • Everything is hitters, almost. The pitcher side received one afternoon and immediately showed a larger expected-statistic edge than the hitter side. It is the most obviously under-explored area.
  • No interactive exploration. Every analysis was written as a script and executed. Nothing was examined column by column in a live session, so column definitions rest on acceptance tests against published figures (§2) rather than on inspection.
  • Streak studies drop streaks with no qualified follow-up — benchings, injuries, demotions — and hot streaks are selected partly on a low baseline, so the shares kept in §4.4 are if anything slightly overstated.

7.2Open questions

  1. Why does the hitter gap persist across seasons? It is real, replicated over three year-pairs at roughly +0.27, it does not appear for pitchers, and no mechanism tested so far explains it. This is the best open question in the project.
  2. Benchmark against Steamer and ZiPS. Requires their pre-season files specifically; the continuously-updated versions already contain the season being forecast and would leak it into the benchmark.
  3. Test whether the season-level failure is sample size. Backfilling 2015–2022 would roughly quintuple the training set and settle it.
  4. Build the slump diagnostic. §4.3 establishes that the skills persist and the results do not. The usable product is a tool that, given a slumping hitter, reports which of his measurements have actually moved. Since built: the dashboard's Player tab (Is his recent form real?, Swing & approach changes).
  5. Compute true trajectory separation at the hitter's commit point and re-test tunnelling honestly.
  6. Test the count reframing. If the count dominates hitter identity by a factor of two, pitch selection is a count-management problem. Nothing has been done here.
  7. Run the whole battery on pitchers.
  8. Replicate the young-hitter result prospectively on a season it did not generate.
  9. Fix the head-to-head drift term (§4.8.2): pre-register a sub-√t or mean-reverting drift and test it on 2027.
  10. Run Marcel as a pairwise forecaster on the identical pairs of §4.8.
  11. Test hard-hit-backed streaks against 2027 (§4.4.1), rather than on the seasons that suggested them.
  12. Explain why late-season streaks keep less — September call-up pitching, fatigue or roster decisions (§4.4.3).
  13. Measure per-player outcome variance from pitch-level data (§5.5).
