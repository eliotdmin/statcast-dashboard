What We Learned and What We Got Wrong

A full account of every question this project asked of four seasons of pitch-level baseball data: what was tested, how, what came back, and the twenty-three occasions on which an answer turned out to be wrong.

Section 1

The project and the data

Since 2015, every pitch thrown in a Major League Baseball game has been tracked by a camera and radar system called Statcast. It records how fast the pitch left the hand, where it crossed the plate, how much it moved, and — if the batter swings — how fast the bat was moving, how hard the ball was struck, at what angle, and where it landed. Since 2024 it also records the geometry of the swing itself: the upward tilt of the bat at contact, its horizontal direction, and the point in space where bat met ball.

This project holds four complete regular seasons of that data — 2023 through 2026 — in a single local database. That is 2,843,142 pitches across 119 columns, of which roughly 486,000 are balls put in play and 1.33 million are tracked swings. The database is 1.8 gigabytes and lives on one laptop.

The animating question was simple and, it turned out, harder than it looked: can this data tell you something about a hitter or pitcher that his statistics alone do not? Baseball has an obvious version of this problem. A hitter goes 2-for-30 over ten days. Is he hurt, is he doing something different with his swing, or has he simply been unlucky? The traditional answer was to wait and see. Statcast offers the possibility of an answer now, because it measures what the hitter did rather than only what happened afterwards.

Everything below is an attempt to establish whether that possibility is real. The short answer, stated up front so that nothing here reads as a slow reveal, is: much less than we expected, in a narrower set of circumstances than we expected, and several of the things we initially believed turned out to be mistakes of our own making.

Section 2

The methods, explained from scratch

Five ideas do almost all of the work in this report. They are worth understanding before the results, because in several cases the method is the finding.

Reliability: does a statistic repeat itself?

Suppose you want to know whether "ground-ball rate" is a real property of a hitter or mostly an accident of which balls happened to be hit on the ground. Split his season into two halves that ought to be equivalent — we used odd-numbered calendar months against even-numbered ones — compute the statistic in each half, and correlate the two halves across all hitters.

If the correlation is near 1, the statistic repeats: hitters who ground out a lot in April also do so in May, and the differences between them are real. If it is near 0, the two halves are unrelated and the entire apparent spread between players was noise. Because each half contains only half the data, this understates the reliability of the full season, and a correction called Spearman–Brown restores it.

This single number decides whether a statistic can support a claim about an individual player. A statistic with reliability 0.16 cannot, no matter how large the difference between two players looks.

Signal share: how much of a change is real?

Every measured quantity is the truth plus an error, and variances add: var(observed) = var(true) + var(noise). The noise part can be computed from sample sizes alone — for a rate it is p(1−p)/n — so the true spread can be recovered by subtraction without ever observing a true value.

Applied to changes rather than levels, this answers the question every "breakout" article dodges: if a hitter's ground-ball rate fell ten points, how much of that was real? We computed the answer for every metric in the dataset, and the ordering turned out to be the single most useful thing the project produced.

Shrinkage: why you should believe less than you see

If a metric is only 30% signal, then an observed change of ten points should be reported as three. Formally the estimate is pulled toward the league average by a factor var(true) / (var(true) + standard error²).

This is not a conservatism heuristic. Charles Stein proved in 1961 that for three or more quantities, the obvious estimator — just use what you measured — is always beaten by one that shrinks toward a common centre. It was so counterintuitive that it is still called Stein's paradox. In 1977 Efron and Morris illustrated it using the batting averages of eighteen players after forty-five at-bats, shrinking them toward the league mean to predict the rest of the season; the shrunk estimates cut total squared error by roughly a factor of 3.5. As section 7 records, the central empirical finding of this project turned out to be a rediscovery of that result.

The noise ceiling: what is the best a forecaster could do?

Forecast accuracy is usually reported as R², the fraction of variation explained, where 1.0 is perfection. But if the thing you are predicting is mostly coin flips, perfection is unreachable and R² near 1 is not the right benchmark.

We derived the real ceiling. For two months of the same hitter in the same season, E[(w₁−w₂)²] = σ²(1/pa₁ + 1/pa₂) + var(drift). Regressing 16,228 squared differences on the sample-size term identifies both pieces: the per-plate-appearance outcome variance is 0.2258, real month-to-month movement in skill has a standard deviation of .038, and true talent across 670 hitters has a spread of just .035.

At a typical month of 87 plate appearances, that means a forecaster who knew every hitter's true talent exactly would still only score R² = 0.264, because the target is mostly noise. Every forecasting number in section 6 should be read against 0.264, not against 1.0. This changes the interpretation of every result completely: a model scoring 0.057 is not failing, it is capturing a fifth of everything available.

Holdout testing and pre-registration

Any model can be made to fit data it has already seen. Every forecasting result here was fit on the 2023–2025 seasons and evaluated exactly once on 2026, which the model never saw during development. Uncertainty is reported as bootstrap intervals: resample the test rows three thousand times with replacement, recompute the whole statistic each time, and report the middle 95%.

Where a hypothesis had a predicted direction, that direction was written down before the query ran. Five predictions were pre-registered in this way. Three of them failed, and they are reported at the same length as the successes.

Section 3

Pitching: mixing, sequencing, fatigue, tunnelling

Four pieces of pitching orthodoxy were tested. Three produced nothing, and the fourth was real but not for the reason usually given.

Does mixing your pitches work? null

Every broadcast says a pitcher must "keep hitters honest" by varying what he throws. We measured the variety of a pitcher's arsenal using entropy — a pitcher who throws 40/30/20/10 has an effective arsenal of about 3.6 pitches, not four — and asked whether broader mixing predicted better results. It did not.

That null was unsatisfying enough that we re-ran it under the conditions most favourable to the hypothesis. If variety matters anywhere, it should matter the third time through the batting order, when a hitter has already seen you twice in the same game. Across 337 starter-seasons, the relationship between arsenal breadth and the third-time-through penalty was −0.0027 ± 0.0040 — an interval spanning zero in both directions against a league spread of 0.074.

A negative result that survives being deliberately re-run under favourable conditions is worth considerably more than the original. The practical reading: a fifth pitch is not what keeps a starter alive in the sixth inning.

The third time through the order is real holds

The penalty itself is genuine and sizeable. Measured within pitcher — comparing each pitcher against his own average, so that "good pitchers are allowed to go deeper" cannot manufacture the effect — a plate appearance is worth .0094 below that pitcher's norm the first time through and .0146 above it the third. That is a swing of 24 points of wOBA from the first pass to the third.

Tunnelling null

The theory is that two pitches released from the same point but finishing in different places are harder to distinguish, so the second should generate extra swings and misses. We examined every pair of consecutive different pitch types within a plate appearance, bucketed by the distance between release points, and compared the whiff rate against the baseline for that exact combination of previous pitch, current pitch, and count.

Across 340,000 swings the excess whiff rate wandered between −0.24 and +0.14 percentage points with no ordering at all. Nothing.

An important caveat we did not resolve. Release-point distance is a crude proxy. The quantity that actually matters is how far apart the two trajectories are at the hitter's commit point, roughly 23 feet from the plate, which is computable from the position, velocity and acceleration vectors in this data and was never computed. So what was shown is that release-point similarity does nothing; "tunnelling does not work" is a stronger claim than the evidence supports.

Velocity decay, and a lesson worth more than the result null

Do starters who lose more velocity over a start get punished for it? The naive approach sorts 492 starts by how fast the fastball was falling and compares early to late results. It produces a beautiful, monotone, wrong answer: across five quintiles the correlation between decay and the late-game penalty was +0.905 — in the opposite direction to the hypothesis, apparently showing that pitchers who gained velocity collapsed hardest.

The fifth row explains it. That group's early wOBA was .2497, wildly better than everyone else's. Those are starts where a pitcher was dominating, was left in, and was still airing it out in the seventh. Their late-game "collapse" is regression from an unsustainable start.

Compared within pitcher — each of his starts against his own average, 8,250 starts from 307 pitchers — the effect is +0.0031 ± 0.0024, indistinguishable from zero and with the sign flipped. We keep this as the project's canonical example that monotonicity across five bins is not evidence of causation; it is what selection effects usually look like.

Section 4

The strike zone and the 2026 challenge system

In 2026 Major League Baseball introduced a challenge system allowing teams to appeal ball and strike calls to automated tracking. This is a large, recent, externally-imposed change to the game — close to a natural experiment — so we measured what it did to the shape of the called zone.

The first attempt produced a confident, wrong answer, and the way it went wrong is instructive enough to describe in full. We measured called-strike rate in bands defined relative to sz_top, the data's own field for the top of each batter's strike zone. It reported that the top of the zone had expanded dramatically, by 32.8 percentage points.

What actually happened

A check on the reference column itself: sz_top had 321,120 distinct values across the league in 2024 and 353,643 in 2025 — consistent with a human operator estimating it for every pitch. In 2026 it had 390, exactly one per batter, and averaged 2.6 inches lower. MLB had replaced the per-pitch estimate with a formula. Measuring 2026 against sz_top and comparing to 2025 was comparing against a different ruler.

Redone in absolute feet above the ground — which the rulebook fixes and no data provider can redefine — the zone changed in the opposite direction at the top: holds

The zone became shorter, deeper, narrower and much crisper. The boundary itself sharpened: the width of the band over which called-strike rate falls from 80% to 20% — a direct measure of how fuzzy the zone is — narrowed from 2.61 inches to 1.90 on the outside edge.

This also explains a number we had produced days earlier and not understood. The spread between the best and worst pitch-framing catchers collapsed from 15.5 runs to 10.6. A crisper zone leaves less to frame.

Section 5

Hitting: two strikes, platoons, and measurement

Choking up is real, nearly universal, and appears to work holds

Bat tracking makes it possible to measure, for the first time at scale, whether hitters actually shorten their swing with two strikes. They do. Across 521 hitter-seasons, average bat speed with two strikes is 1.37 mph slower than without, and 89% of hitters do it.

The size of the adjustment varies a lot between hitters, and those who shorten up most strike out less, whiff less, and produce more in two-strike counts. All three correlations clear their intervals.

This is not causal and we did not make it so. Hitters with better bat control can afford to shorten up and are also better contact hitters anyway, so the adjustment may be a marker of a skill rather than its cause. The discriminating test — does the same hitter do better in the seasons he shortens up more — was specified and never run.

Platoon splits are a real individual trait holds

The average hitter is 31 points of wOBA better against opposite-handed pitching. The spread between hitters in how large their split is comes out at 0.0414, of which sampling noise accounts for 0.0217, leaving a true spread of 0.0353 — 72% signal.

So "he handles same-handed pitching unusually well" is a real, measurable property. But 28% of the apparent spread on any published platoon leaderboard is noise, which means the extremes are systematically overstated. A hitter shown with a 90-point split most likely has a true split nearer 70. This matters operationally, because platoon splits drive pinch-hitting and bullpen decisions in live games, and the operational version rarely shrinks.

Bat speed is not measured identically in every ballpark holds

We compared each team's own hitters at home against the same hitters on the road. Same players, different sensor installations, so any systematic gap is the ballpark's tracking rather than the hitting. Home-minus-road bat speed varies across the thirty parks with a standard deviation of 0.23 mph, and home-minus-road exit velocity with a standard deviation of 0.46.

The tell is that the two are correlated at +0.456. Bat speed and exit velocity are separately derived measurements; a park where both read high, for visiting and home hitters alike, is a park whose installation runs hot. Against a true year-over-year bat-speed spread of 1.01 mph, a 0.23 mph measurement artifact is not negligible for the many changes in the 0.3–0.6 range that this project had been treating as marginal but real.

The calibration table, which became the project's most-used output holds

Applying the signal-share method of section 2 to every metric, across 222 hitters with 250+ plate appearances in both 2025 and 2026, produced an ordering that reframed everything after it:

The ordering is the lesson. Everything a hitter does — how fast and how long he swings, whether he swings — is measurable in a single season. Everything that depends on what happened to the ball afterwards is mostly luck at one season of sample. Sweet-spot rate has literally no detectable year-over-year signal across the league: the entire apparent spread is sampling noise. Any story about a hitter changing his launch angle profile, built on one season of sweet-spot rate, is a story about nothing.

On the pitching side the split is sharper still. Arm angle, release extension, fastball velocity and spin rate are all 97–100% signal, while walk rate and edge rate are 1%. A pitcher's body is precisely measured; his command is not.

Section 6

Forecasting, the main line of work

The largest single effort went into one question: given what a hitter did in one stretch, how well can you predict what he does in the next? This is the practical question behind every roster decision, and it is the question on which expected statistics were supposed to deliver.

Two quantities matter throughout. wOBA (weighted on-base average) assigns each plate-appearance outcome its average run value, scaled to look like on-base percentage; it is a description of what happened. Expected wOBA replaces each batted ball's actual outcome with the league-average result of balls hit at the same exit velocity and launch angle, leaving strikeouts and walks alone; it is a description of how the ball was struck, deliberately blind to where it was hit, to the ballpark and to the defence.

At one month, using what a hitter just hit to predict what he hits next is worse than predicting league average for everybody. Expected wOBA is better at every horizon.

Two things we predicted in advance turned out wrong. We expected expected-wOBA's advantage to shrink as the window lengthened, since wOBA's own noise falls with sample. Instead it is humped: it rises from one month to two, holds through three, and nearly vanishes over a full season. We have a story for this but not a mechanism, and the three-month row has the smallest test sample. prediction failed

We also expected the optimal weight on expected wOBA to fall as sample grew. Instead, fitting both together within buckets of playing time, the coefficient on actual wOBA is negative in every bucket above 55 plate appearances. Conditional on how the ball was struck, what actually happened is not merely uninformative — it points the wrong way. A hitter who beat his expected output should be marked down. prediction failed

A richer model helps, and one of its best features is embarrassing holds

Adding contact quality, plate discipline, batted-ball mix, context and swing geometry — 48 features in total — raises one-month accuracy from 0.015 to 0.057, or about a fifth of the theoretical ceiling. Swing geometry earns its place: adding attack angle, swing tilt, contact depth and bat speed produces the single largest jump after context, and in the simplified linear version bat speed carries the second-largest coefficient of any input, ahead of expected wOBA itself.

The uncomfortable control

Plate appearances turned up as the second most important feature, so we ran it alone. How often a manager writes a hitter into the lineup predicts next month better than how hard he hits the ball — 0.032 against expected wOBA's 0.015. That is not a hitting statistic. It is the manager's private information about health, matchups and what he has seen in person. It is retained in headline numbers because a real forecaster would observe it too, but it is now always reported alongside the version without it.

The trade-off nobody states holds

A statistic can describe what happened, agree with its own next measurement, or forecast the future. These are different properties and they trade off almost perfectly:

Read down the columns: the better a metric describes the month that just happened, the worse it forecasts the next one. wOBA is the perfect description by construction and the worst available forecaster, because a perfect description includes all of the noise.

Section 7

Marcel, and what happened when we met the baseline

All of the above was encouraging until we compared it to the correct benchmark.

Marcel — named after the monkey from Friends, and published by Tom Tango in 2004 precisely so that people with complicated systems would have something to clear — does three things. It averages a hitter's last three seasons weighted 5/4/3. It regresses that average hard toward the league mean by adding a block of fictional league-average plate appearances. And it nudges the result for age. It has no batted-ball data, no tracking data, and no model. It is about five lines of arithmetic.

Before comparing, we made two corrections against our own models, because the alternative is a strawman. Marcel's regression constant was tuned on training years, exactly as our models' hyperparameters were. And every model was re-run with the same career history Marcel sees, since we had handicapped ours to a single window.

Result: a draw, and then a loss

At a one-month horizon, tuned Marcel scores 0.0584 and our 48-feature gradient-boosted model scores 0.0565. The difference is −0.002 [−0.039, +0.033]; the probability our model is better is 0.46. Giving the models Marcel's history does not change it. Eight model families were eventually tried — ridge with a cross-validated penalty of 56,000, elastic net, partial least squares, principal components, and three variants that use Marcel as an offset and model only its residual. Not one of them beats it.

The Marcel-as-offset result is the most informative. If tracking data held information Marcel lacks, modelling Marcel's residual is exactly where it would appear. It does not appear.

On Marcel's home ground — forecasting a full season from prior seasons — it is worse than a draw. Tuned Marcel scores 0.164. A twenty-feature model built on past Statcast scores −0.060, meaning it is worse than predicting league average for everyone. With 267 training seasons and twenty correlated predictors, that is a textbook variance failure. Meanwhile a plain shrunk average of past expected wOBA ties Marcel exactly, 0.166 to 0.164.

The value is in the shrinking, not in the features.

We then tried to build a better Marcel, testing four evidence-backed improvements in sequence. Only one worked, and it was not a feature. Changing the regression constant from the classic 1200 to a tuned 3000 moved season accuracy from 0.0745 to 0.1640 — a bigger gain than every Statcast feature in this project combined. The reason is stark: a hitter's weighted three-year record correlates with his next season at only 0.40, so roughly 60% of his apparent deviation from league average should be discarded even with two thousand plate appearances of history. Classic Marcel keeps 87%.

Where measurement does win tentative

One segment survives. Marcel's blind spot is a player it has never seen: with no history it can only predict league average. Splitting the test set by how much prior playing time a hitter has, the model beats Marcel by +0.049 in the bottom quartile and loses by 0.040 in the top, monotonically across all four. By age, the only segment whose interval excludes zero is 22 to 25 years old, where the model wins by +0.065.

An important correction to the natural summary: expected wOBA alone loses to Marcel in every age bucket, including the youngest. What beats Marcel is the full measurement model, not the expected statistic. And this was a post-hoc split across eight segments, where one interval excluding zero is roughly what chance produces. It is a hypothesis for next season, not a finding.

We also predicted the advantage would concentrate among hitters whose measurements had just moved — Marcel literally cannot see a three-mph bat-speed gain. Among the top decile of movers the gap was −0.001, probability 0.50. Exactly nothing. The blind spot we were most confident about does not matter at this horizon. prediction failed

Section 8

The gap between what happened and what should have

When a hitter's actual wOBA exceeds his expected wOBA, the conventional reading is luck. We spent several days on whether that is true, arrived at a confident mechanistic answer, and then had to withdraw it.

Within a season the gap behaves like noise. Its split-half reliability is 0.164, near the bottom of everything we measured — against 0.978 for bat speed, 0.879 for exit velocity and 0.448 for wOBA itself. A statistic that repeats with itself that weakly cannot support a claim about any individual hitter.

Across seasons, however, it persists. The same gap correlates with itself at +0.267, +0.302 and +0.238 over three independent year-pairs. Those two facts were in tension, and the tension was the interesting part.

The explanation we published and withdrew

We found that expected wOBA under-rated ground balls by 23 points per batted ball and that roughly half of that was reached-on-error — a routine grounder that a fielder mishandles, which the model scores as an out while the batter stands on first. It was specific, quantified, and it explained the named over-performers (Altuve, Friedl, Trea Turner — fast ground-ball hitters) and under-performers (Soto, Guerrero — slow air-ball hitters).

It was wrong. The reader who commissioned this report asked whether actual wOBA really credits a batter for reaching on an error. It does not — wOBA, like on-base percentage, treats it as an out. But the pitch-level woba_value column in this data does: it carries 0.900 for a fielding error, 0.900 for a fielder's choice, and 0.700 for a dropped third strike. That column records whether the batter reached base, not wOBA. Every wOBA computed in this project was inflated by about nine points, and the "finding" was our own numerator measured against a correctly specified expected wOBA.

Corrected, the ground-ball gap is +0.0008. The effect disappears entirely, and so does a supposed league-wide calibration offset that had appeared in every count.

What survives the correction: expected wOBA genuinely over-rates popups, by 17.6 points per batted ball, though popups are only 7% of batted balls. And the cross-season persistence survives intact, which means it was never an error artifact. So the gap really does persist for hitters, and we no longer have an explanation for it.

One clean asymmetry did emerge and is worth keeping. Run identically on 1,335 pitcher-seasons, the gap does not persist: +0.104, −0.021, +0.161 across the same three year-pairs, and a split-half reliability of 0.213. Whatever drives the hitter version is a batter attribute. A pitcher does not choose his launch angles and does not run to first base.

On pitchers more generally, expected wOBA against beats actual wOBA against at predicting next season by 34% in relative terms — a much larger edge than the hitter version. And strikeout rate and walk rate alone reach 0.156 against expected wOBA's 0.170, using no batted-ball data whatsoever. That is the DIPS result from 1999, reproduced with modern data: what a pitcher controls is mostly the outcomes that never become a batted ball.

On expected ERA, which is the natural next question: it is not in this archive, and it would add nothing. Savant's own glossary defines it as "a simple 1:1 translation of xwOBA, converted to the ERA scale." It is a rescaling and carries no independent information. A structural decomposition of expected ERA against ERA would be the analysis above plus sequencing, bullpen inheritance, and the official scorer's earned-versus-unearned judgement, which this data does not contain at all.

Section 9

The count, and the largest effect we found

The question was posed plainly: if a plate appearance arrives at a given count, what does it eventually produce — counting everything that happens afterwards, not merely contact in that count. The range from the worst count to the best is .351 of wOBA. Across 286 hitters with a thousand or more plate appearances in these four seasons, the range from worst to best is .185. The count matters 1.89 times as much as the identity of the hitter, and on standard deviations rather than ranges the factor is four.

Put concretely: an average major-league hitter, at 3–0, out-produces the best hitter in baseball. The same hitter, at 0–2, is worse than the worst. The first strike is the most expensive single event in the sequence — 0–0 to 0–1 costs .048, and 0–1 to 0–2 costs a further .068.

This is the largest effect in the report and it was the last thing we looked at. It also reframes the pitching work in section 3: if the count dominates hitter identity by that margin, then the value of any individual pitch is mostly its effect on the count, which makes pitch selection a count-management problem rather than a deception problem. That reframing is untested.

Section 10

Slumps: what persists and what does not

The final analysis addresses the question the project began with. A hitter is slumping. What, if anything, does that tell you about next month?

Skills are stable. Results are not. And the gap between the two is enormous: bat speed repeats at .919 over a single month while wOBA repeats at .135.

The sharper version removes each hitter's own career mean, which is what separates the slump from the hitter. Once that is done, the carryover collapses to nothing: wOBA −.038, −.080, −.055 across the three window lengths, and expected wOBA +.004, −.000, +.072.

Caveat, recorded because it matters. Demeaning a short panel induces a mechanical negative bias of roughly −1/(T−1). The small negatives above are therefore consistent with zero carryover rather than genuine anti-persistence, and should be read that way.

The operational conclusion

A slump, measured as a deviation in results from a hitter's own norm, predicts essentially nothing about the following month. But the underlying skills — whiff rate, bat speed, exit velocity — persist strongly. So the correct response to a slumping hitter is not to ask how bad the slump is, but to ask whether anything he does has changed. If his bat speed and whiff rate are where they were, the slump carries no information. If they have moved, that movement is real and will persist.

Section 11

Every error, in full

Twenty-three occasions on which something in this project was wrong. They are listed because the ratio of caught to uncaught errors is the only evidence that the surviving results were checked at all, and because several of them are more instructive than the findings.

Errors of data handling

  • The database was silently corrupted. Building an index across a network mount produced a disk I/O error that was treated as a warning. The result under-counted by 23,523 pitches with no visible symptom. Recovered in full via SQLite's .recover and a verification script comparing stored rows against the ingest log.
  • A typo put the data range through 2028, a season that has not happened. Caught by the reader, not by us.
  • A command-line flag of 0 was falsy, so a limit of zero days fetched everything instead of nothing.
  • The audit ran 476 full table scans where 24 would do, by not grouping columns that share an applicability rule.
  • Player names were pulled from the wrong column. In pitch-level data player_name is the pitcher. A hitter report printed bare numeric IDs. The data dictionary warns about this in bold, which we had written ourselves.

Errors of definition — the expensive category

  • The wOBA column is not wOBA. Described in section 8. Four days of analysis inherited a nine-point inflation and one published finding was entirely an artifact of it. Caught by a reader question about whether on-base percentage rises on an error.
  • The strike-zone reference column changed meaning between seasons. Described in section 4. Produced a confident conclusion in exactly the wrong direction.
  • A regression of the wOBA gap on ground-ball-single rate returned R² = 0.246 and meant nothing. A ground ball that finds a hole is simultaneously a ground-ball single and a positive gap — the same event on both sides of the equation. Ground-ball single rate has a split-half reliability of 0.193, so this was luck explaining luck.
  • A gap regression reported R² = 0.719 by comparing full-season wOBA against contact-only expected wOBA. Corrected to 0.133.

Errors of inference

  • Leakage in our own benchmark. Season-level Marcel scored R² = 0.575 and the tuner chose "barely regress" — both symptoms of a history window that included the season being forecast. The true value is 0.164. The diagnostic is now a standing rule: a baseline suddenly outperforming its published behaviour, combined with tuning that says not to regress, is leakage until proven otherwise.
  • A verdict recorded on the wrong evidence. "Chase rate is a fast-moving discipline signal" was scored as confirmed because chase-rate changes were detectable. Detectability is not predictive value. Tested properly, this month's change in chase rate predicts next month's change in production at +0.046 ± 0.036 — nothing — while the same-month association is −0.370 ± 0.041. Chase rate is coincident, not leading.
  • A hypothesis was silently dropped. One of the pre-registered tests never appeared in the scorecard. Run later, it returned a clean null.
  • A positive control was overclaimed as showing no effect, when what it showed was an inability to detect an effect below a certain size.
  • A recommendation was reversed within a day. We advised that bat speed would work at monthly resolution because it is highly reliable as a level. The reliability of a level is not the reliability of a change: monthly bat-speed change is 34% signal against 85% seasonally.
  • A mechanism was built on without checking its size. The ground-ball effect, before it was retracted on other grounds, was worth 0.0014 of wOBA per standard deviation of ground-ball rate against a gap spread of 0.0188 — 7.3% of the thing it purported to explain. A one-line arithmetic check would have shown this before the modelling rather than after.
  • A model was misspecified in a way that inverted a coefficient. Regressing the Marcel residual on batted-ball profile made ground-ball rate load negative, because the residual contains overall hitter quality and ground-ball hitters are worse hitters. It was measuring the wrong thing entirely.
  • A pre-registered prediction about the 2026 rule change was wrong on the rule. We predicted effects of full automation; 2026 is a challenge system. The rule should have been checked before the prediction was written.

Errors of scope and communication

  • An artifact was published claiming a win it had not earned. The forecasting page stated that the model separates future performance four times better than actual wOBA, which is true, before any benchmark existed. Marcel separates it 3.8 times better with none of the machinery. The page was revised rather than left standing.
  • A correct narrow finding was used to justify a wrong broad conclusion. That batted-ball rates are low signal year over year was used to argue that batted-ball outcomes were not worth studying. The correct statement is far narrower: a player-season change in a batted-ball rate is low signal, because one hitter records roughly 450 balls in play a year. At the unit of the batted ball there are 486,000 observations. The aggregation was the problem, not the data.
  • A claim about documentation was made without checking. We asserted that the data provider published no usable glossary. It does, and fetching it corrected four dictionary entries and surfaced the rule change noted above.
  • Unverified configuration was shipped as a recommendation, with the verification left to the reader. Withdrawn and replaced after testing.
  • Two mutually inconsistent ceilings were reported in different documents, 0.186 and 0.315, because one included month-to-month drift in the denominator and the other did not. Reconciled at 0.264.

Predictions that failed

Five directional predictions were registered before their tests ran. Three were wrong: that expected wOBA's advantage would decline monotonically with window length (it is humped); that the weight on expected wOBA would fall as sample grew (the weight on actual wOBA went negative); and that the measurement models would beat Marcel most for hitters whose measurements had just moved (no effect at all). Recording these is the only thing that makes the two that succeeded worth anything.

Section 12

Shortcomings

  • One test year. Every forecasting number rests on the 2026 holdout. Season-level results rest on 245 hitters in a single season. The bootstrap intervals reflect sampling within that year, not the year-to-year variability of the whole exercise.
  • A four-season archive is a short runway. The season-level model had 267 training rows where professional systems train on twenty years. Its failure is most plausibly a sample-size failure rather than a verdict on the features, but that is an explanation and not a test.
  • Swing tracking cannot ever have a long history. It begins in 2024. Even backfilling to 2015 would give exit velocity eight more seasons and swing geometry none.
  • Marcel is the floor, not the standard. Steamer and ZiPS are substantially stronger and neither was tested; access to their pre-season files is gated. "No better than the baseline" is therefore a weaker statement than it sounds.
  • Survivorship in the panel. Requiring forty plate appearances in both the input and target window conditions on continued playing time, which is itself informative about performance.
  • Hyperparameters were fixed by judgement, not tuned, and model selection was not nested inside the bootstrap, so the intervals do not account for specification search.
  • Post-hoc segmentation. The age and history splits were run after the main result was known. Eight segments were examined; one interval excluding zero is approximately what chance produces.
  • Several causal questions were left as correlations — most notably whether shortening the swing with two strikes causes better two-strike results.
  • Everything is hitters, almost. The pitcher side received one afternoon and immediately showed a larger expected-statistic edge than the hitter side. It is the most obviously under-explored area.
  • No interactive exploration. Every analysis was written as a script and executed. Nothing was examined column by column in a live session, which is precisely how the wOBA definition error survived for four days.

Section 13

What is still open

  1. Why does the hitter gap persist across seasons? It is real, replicated over three year-pairs at roughly +0.27, it does not appear for pitchers, and the explanation we proposed has been withdrawn. This is the best open question in the project.
  2. Benchmark against Steamer and ZiPS. Requires their pre-season files specifically; the continuously-updated versions already contain the season being forecast and would reproduce a leak we have already made once.
  3. Test whether the season-level failure is sample size. Backfilling 2015–2022 would roughly quintuple the training set and settle it.
  4. Build the slump diagnostic. Section 10 establishes that the skills persist and the results do not. The usable product is a tool that, given a slumping hitter, reports which of his measurements have actually moved.
  5. Compute true trajectory separation at the hitter's commit point and re-test tunnelling honestly.
  6. Test the count reframing. If the count dominates hitter identity by a factor of two, pitch selection is a count-management problem. Nothing has been done here.
  7. Run the whole battery on pitchers.
  8. Replicate the young-hitter result prospectively on a season it did not generate.
