Forecasting Hitters from Short Samples

A formal record of the forecasting work: estimators, assumptions, hypotheses with their pre-registration status, test statistics with intervals, and a log of the errors found and corrected. Companion to the interactive instrument; this document is the arithmetic, not the argument.

abstract

Using 10,287 hitter-months of Statcast data (2023–2026), we ask whether expected statistics forecast future production better than realised ones, whether a richer feature set improves on them, and what explains the residual between the two. At a one-month horizon, realised wOBA has negative out-of-sample R² (−0.004); xwOBA reaches 0.015; a 48-feature gradient-boosted model reaches 0.057. All three are dominated by the noise floor: at the mean test sample of 87 plate appearances, a forecaster with perfect knowledge of stable talent attains R² = 0.264, so the best model captures 21% of what is attainable.

Benchmarked against Marcel — a 2004 three-year weighted average with regression to the mean and an age adjustment — every model is statistically indistinguishable (48-feature model minus Marcel: −0.002, 95% CI [−0.039, +0.033]). At season level Marcel is superior to a Statcast-history model (0.164 vs −0.060). The exception is hitters aged 22–25, where the model exceeds Marcel by +0.065 (CI [+0.003, +0.125]).

The wOBA–xwOBA residual is not a within-season skill (split-half ρ = 0.164) but is weakly persistent across seasons (r = +0.27 to +0.30 over three year-pairs), driven by foot speed and pull tendency; exit velocity carries a negative coefficient at every window length. Three pre-registered predictions failed and are reported as such.

1.2Scope limits that bind the conclusions

  1. The archive begins in 2023. Nothing here observes a hitter before that season. Any Marcel-style forecast needs three prior years, so only the 2026 season can be forecast at season level, giving a single test year (n = 245). This is the binding constraint on every season-level result and the most likely explanation for one of them (§6.2).
  2. Bat tracking does not exist before 2024. attack_angle, attack_direction, swing_path_tilt and contact-point coordinates are half-populated in 2023 and complete thereafter. Swing-geometry features are therefore a 2024–2026 asset and can never acquire a long history, regardless of further backfill.
  3. 2026 is a temporal holdout and was never used for fitting, tuning, or feature selection in any arm.
  4. No sprint speed, no umpire identity, no fielder coordinates. The sprint_speed table holds 552 rows (one season); umpire is an empty column. Fielding alignment (if_fielding_alignment) is present on 99.8% of batted balls and is used.

2Definitions and estimators

2.1wOBA and xwOBA

wOBA (weighted on-base average) assigns each plate-appearance outcome its average run value, scaled to the on-base-percentage range. It is a descriptive statistic: it records what happened. xwOBA replaces each batted ball's actual outcome with the league-average wOBA of balls hit at the same exit velocity and launch angle (estimated_woba_using_speedangle), leaving strikeouts and walks as they are. It is therefore a statement about how the ball was struck, deliberately blind to where it was hit, to the park, and to the defence.

wOBA = Σ w(outcome) / PA
xwOBA = Σ E[w | exit velocity, launch angle] / PA
both summed over plate appearances with woba_denom = 1

2.2Split-half reliability and Spearman–Brown

Reliability asks how much of the spread in a measured statistic is real and how much is sampling noise. Operationally: split a player's season into two halves that should be equivalent — here, odd-numbered calendar months against even-numbered ones — compute the statistic in each, and correlate the two halves across players.

A correlation of 1 means the statistic is perfectly repeatable and every difference between players is real. A correlation of 0 means the two halves are unrelated and the entire apparent spread was noise. Because each half has only half the data, this understates the reliability of the full season, and the Spearman–Brown correction restores it:

ρfull = 2r / (1 + r)   where r is the half-to-half correlation

Equivalently: reliability = var(true) / var(observed). It is the fraction of the variance in what you measured that reflects genuine differences between players. This is the quantity that decides whether a statistic is worth discussing at all. A statistic with reliability 0.16 cannot support a claim about any individual, however large the number looks.

Two caveats on the odd/even split. It assumes the halves are exchangeable, which fails if a hitter genuinely changed mid-season — real change is then scored as unreliability. And it assumes both halves have similar sample sizes; Spearman–Brown is derived for parallel tests of equal length. Both caveats push the estimate down, so the reliabilities in Table 6 are lower bounds.

2.3Variance decomposition and signal share

For any observed quantity that is a true value plus independent sampling error, variances add:

var(observed) = var(true) + var(noise)
signal share = var(true) / var(observed) = 1 − var(noise)/var(observed)

var(noise) is computable from sample sizes alone — for a rate, p(1−p)/n; for a mean, s²/n — so the true spread is recoverable by subtraction without ever observing a true value. Applied to changes rather than levels, the same identity gives the fraction of an observed year-over-year change worth believing.

2.4Shrinkage

The signal share is also the optimal multiplier on an observed deviation. For an observation with standard error se drawn from a population with true variance σ²true:

estimate = league mean + (observed − league mean) × k
k = σ²true / (σ²true + se²)

This is the posterior mean under a normal prior — empirical Bayes — and is the estimator Stein (1961) proved dominates the raw observation for three or more parameters. Efron and Morris (1977) illustrated it using the batting averages of 18 players after 45 at-bats, reducing total squared error against rest-of-season by roughly a factor of 3.5. The principal empirical finding of this project (§6.2) is a rediscovery of that result.

2.5Evaluation metric

All forecasts are scored by out-of-sample R² against the test set's own weighted mean, weighted by the target window's plate appearances so that a 40-PA month does not count as much as a 120-PA one:

R² = 1 − Σ wi(yi − Ŷi)² / Σ wi(yi − ȳw)²   wi = target-window PA

A negative value therefore means the forecaster is worse than assuming every hitter is exactly league average. Intervals are percentile bootstraps over test rows, 3,000 resamples, resampling the row and recomputing the whole statistic including the denominator. Head-to-head gaps are computed within each resample, which is the paired comparison and is far tighter than differencing two independent intervals.

3The predictability ceiling

R² against a one-month target is bounded well below 1 because the target is mostly sampling noise. Establishing that bound is a precondition for interpreting any of the results.

For two months of the same hitter within one season, assume the observed rates differ by true drift plus independent sampling error. Then:

E[(w1 − w2)²] = σ²(1/pa1 + 1/pa2) + var(Δtrue)
regress the squared differences on (1/pa₁ + 1/pa₂):
slope = σ², intercept = var(Δtrue)

Estimated on 16,228 within-season month pairs (both months 30+ PA):

At the test set's mean target of 87 plate appearances, sampling noise is more than twice the combined variance of talent and drift. Two ceilings follow:

R²stable = var(talent) / [var(talent) + var(drift) + σ²/n] = 0.264
R²oracle = [var(talent) + var(drift)] / [same denominator] = 0.425

The first is the realistic ceiling: a forecaster with perfect knowledge of every hitter's stable talent, unable to foresee which month he will run hot. The second is unattainable — it requires foreseeing the drift itself. Every R² in §6 should be read against 0.264, not against 1.0.

4Forecasters compared

4.1Marcel

Tango (2004). Three steps, no batted-ball data:

1. weighted record = Σj=1..3 wj·numy−j / Σj wj·PAy−j,   w = (5, 4, 3)
2. regressed = (Σw·num + R·lg) / (Σw·PA + R)
3. × (1 + (29 − age)·0.006) if age < 29, else × (1 + (29 − age)·0.003)

R is the regression constant, classically 1200. Here it is tuned on training years only (grid 200–3000) so that the baseline receives the same courtesy as the challengers; comparing a tuned model to an untuned baseline is a strawman. The tuner selected R = 3000 at season level and R = 1200 with xwOBA history at month level. The monthly variant adds the current season through the input month at the top weight.

4.2Statcast models

Nested feature sets: base (wOBA, xwOBA) → contact quality (8) → plate discipline (8) → batted-ball mix (8) → speed proxies (3) → context and playing time (10) → swing geometry (9); 48 total. Three learners: ridge with generalised cross-validated α; histogram gradient boosting (350 iterations, depth 3, learning rate 0.04, min leaf 40, L2 = 1); random forest (300 trees, min leaf 12). Median imputation fitted on training rows only. A 19-feature weighted-history variant applies Marcel's (5,4,3,2) weights to every Statcast feature rather than to wOBA alone.

The residual is near the bottom: within a season it behaves as luck. O3: nevertheless it correlates with itself across seasons at +0.293, +0.303, +0.274 over three independent year-pairs. These two facts are in tension and the tension is not fully resolved; the within-season estimate is a lower bound (§2.2) and the cross-season figure is measured on larger samples, but the discrepancy is larger than that alone comfortably explains.

P3 confirmed: as the window lengthens the residual shrinks (0.042 → 0.026) while the ante-hoc explainable share quadruples (0.034 → 0.137) — the signature of luck averaging out over a stable physical component. O4: exit velocity carries a negative coefficient at every window length (−0.006 at one month, −0.008 at three) and pull-side spray a positive one. Since xwOBA maps exit velocity and launch angle to a league-average outcome and is blind to direction, it systematically over-credits hard contact from slow hitters and under-credits speed and extreme pull. The persistent over-performers (Altuve +.048, Friedl +.048, Paredes +.040, Turner +.030) and under-performers (S. Perez −.024, Soto −.021, Guerrero −.016) are consistent with this reading.

Controlling for the level of xwOBA, the residual's predictive value for future wOBA is null at one month (−0.038 ± 0.024) and negative at three (−0.107 ± 0.054): over a meaningful stretch, out-performing expectation forecasts giving it back.

The three properties trade off monotonically. A statistic that describes the past perfectly is the worst available forecaster of the future, because a perfect description includes all of the noise.

7Threats to validity

  1. One test year. Every headline figure rests on the 2026 holdout. Season-level results rest on 245 hitters in a single season; the bootstrap intervals reflect sampling within that year but not the year-to-year variability of the whole exercise.
  2. Marcel is handicapped by the archive. It is designed for three prior seasons and gets exactly three, all of which this project also used for training. A longer archive would help Marcel as well as the models, and it is not obvious which benefits more.
  3. Neither Steamer nor ZiPS has been tested. Marcel is the floor of the field, not its standard. The conclusion "no better than the baseline" is therefore weaker than it sounds: the relevant comparison is to systems substantially stronger than Marcel, and remains unrun (§9).
  4. Survivorship in the panel. Requiring 40+ PA in both the input and target window conditions on continued playing time, which is itself informative about performance. This biases all forecasters in the same direction but not necessarily by the same amount.
  5. The odd/even split-half is not a clean instrument for a statistic that genuinely drifts within a season (§2.2), which is precisely the case for the residual in Table 10.
  6. Post-hoc segmentation. O5 and the age × history crossing were run after the main result was known. Four age buckets and four history quartiles is eight tests; one interval excluding zero at the 5% level is roughly what chance produces.
  7. Model selection was not nested inside the bootstrap. Hyperparameters were fixed by judgement rather than tuned, which avoids one form of optimism but means the intervals do not account for specification search.

8Corrections log

Errors found during this work and what was changed. Recorded because the discrimination between these and the surviving results is the only evidence that the surviving results were checked at all.

C1 · leakage in the benchmark

Season-level Marcel initially scored R² = 0.575, with the tuner selecting a regression constant of 200 ("barely regress"). Cause: the history window was specified as thru = 99, which included the season being forecast. Corrected to prior seasons only; the true value is 0.164. Diagnostic retained: a baseline suddenly outperforming its published behaviour, together with tuning that says not to regress, indicates leakage until proven otherwise.

C2 · circular explanation of the residual

The residual regression initially reported R² = 0.246 with ground-ball single rate as the dominant term. A ground ball that finds a hole is simultaneously a ground-ball single and a positive residual: the same event on both sides of the equation. Ground-ball single rate has split-half reliability 0.193, i.e. it is itself mostly luck. Predictors are now partitioned into ante-hoc and outcome-derived and reported separately (Table 11); the honest ante-hoc figure at one month is 0.034.

C3 · two inconsistent ceilings

The ceiling was reported as 0.186 in one place and 0.315 in another. The first included month-to-month drift in the denominator, the second omitted it. Reconciled in §3: 0.264 with drift treated as unforecastable, 0.425 as an unattainable oracle bound.

C4 · a verdict recorded on the wrong evidence

The hypothesis "chase rate is a fast-moving discipline signal" was initially scored confirmed because chase-rate changes were detectable. Detectability is not predictive value. Tested properly — within hitter, 1,119 month-triples — this month's change in chase rate predicts next month's change in wOBA at +0.046 ± 0.036 (null), while the same-month association is −0.370 ± 0.041. Chase rate is coincident, not leading. Rescored.

C5 · a published artifact that overclaimed

The interactive companion was published before the Marcel benchmark existed, stating that the fitted model separates future performance four times better than realised wOBA. True, but incomplete: Marcel separates it 3.8 times better with none of the apparatus. The page was revised rather than left standing.

C6 · a scope error inherited from earlier work

An earlier finding that batted-ball rates are "low signal" was used to argue that batted-ball outcomes were not worth studying. The correct statement is narrower: a player-season change in a batted-ball rate is low signal, because one hitter records roughly 450 balls in play a year. At the unit of the batted ball there are 486,000 observations and the power is ample. The aggregation, not the data, was the problem.

9Open questions

  1. Steamer and ZiPS. The decisive benchmark and the one still missing. It requires pre-season projections — the in-season updated versions already incorporate the target period and would reproduce C1 exactly. FanGraphs publishes both but gates CSV export behind membership.
  2. Is the season-level failure sample size or specification? A 20-feature model at −0.060 on 267 training seasons is the textbook signature of overfitting, but that is an explanation, not a test. Backfilling 2015–2022 would roughly quintuple the training set. Note the asymmetry in §1.2[2]: exit velocity would gain eight seasons, swing geometry none.
  3. Replicate O5 prospectively. The 22–25 result should be treated as a hypothesis and tested on 2027 rather than cited as a finding.
  4. Build the shrinkage estimator rather than the model. If a shrunk average of past xwOBA ties Marcel, the deliverable may be two numbers — a per-metric regression constant and a weighting scheme — rather than a fitted model. This is both more trustworthy and simpler to deploy.
  5. Rest-of-season is the operationally relevant target and has a higher ceiling than next month, since the target is less noisy. Not yet run.
  6. Pitchers are entirely untouched. The prior is a lower ceiling and a larger expected-statistic edge, since pitchers exert less control over batted-ball outcomes.
  7. Resolve the Table 10 tension. A statistic with within-season reliability 0.164 that correlates +0.29 with itself across seasons is not fully explained by the lower-bound argument. A variance-components model with player, season and residual terms would settle it.
