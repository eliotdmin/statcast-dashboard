The Batted Ball Program

Eleven theories about what determines whether a struck ball becomes a run, built on the layer of this dataset we have not touched: 1.0 million tracked swings with the geometry of the bat at contact.

correcting yesterday's framing

I said batted-ball metrics were low signal and should be de-emphasised. What is actually true is narrower: a player's season-to-season change in a batted-ball rate is mostly noise, because one hitter puts about 450 balls in play a year. Ground-ball rate at 18% signal is a statement about 450 events, not about ground balls.

The batted ball itself is a different unit. There are 486,000 of them here, each carrying exit velocity, launch angle, landing coordinates, the pitch that caused it and — from 2024 — the full geometry of the swing that produced it. At that unit the power is enormous. I aggregated away the thing that matters and then blamed the data for being quiet.

the frame

A batted ball is the output of a chain, and every link is measured

Most public batted-ball analysis starts at exit velocity and launch angle, because that is where estimated_woba_using_speedangle starts. That skips the two most interesting links: what the swing did to produce those numbers, and what the park and the defence did to them afterwards. Both are in this database.

Phase A · swing → contact

How the swing makes the ball

This is the part nobody outside a team has done at depth, because the data is eighteen months old. Four theories, all on 2024–2026, all within-hitter.

T1

Attack angle only matters relative to the pitch

high value1.0M swings

The bat's upward tilt at contact is meaningless on its own. What determines whether the ball is squared up is the difference between the bat's attack angle and the pitch's descent angle, and the optimum is a specific positive offset, not zero.

Where this comes from. Ted Williams drew this diagram in The Science of Hitting in 1970 — a slight uppercut matched to the roughly ten-degree downward plane of the pitch. It has been coaching orthodoxy on faith for fifty-five years. Bat tracking shipped in 2024, so this is close to the first moment it can be measured at scale rather than asserted.
  1. Compute the pitch's vertical approach angle at the plate from the trajectory constants: vz_f = −√(vz0² + 2·az·(y0 − 17/12)), then VAA = atan2(vz_f, vy_f) in degrees. Validate before use — league mean four-seam VAA must land near −5°, sinkers steeper. If it does not, the sign convention is wrong and everything downstream is garbage.
  2. Define offset = attack_angle + VAA. Bin in 1° buckets.
  3. Within each bucket compute mean exit velocity, mean launch angle, barrel rate, and xwOBA per swing — whiffs and fouls included at their real value, not dropped. This is the design point the published versions of this analysis get wrong: a steeper swing buys loft and costs contact, and only a per-swing denominator prices both sides.
  4. De-mean offset and outcome within hitter-season, so the curve describes a hitter's own variation rather than which hitters swing which way.
  5. Add controls for pitch location bucket and pitch type — a hitter's offset is correlated with what he chose to swing at, which is a confound, not an effect.
  6. Refit on 2024–2025 only and check the optimum reproduces on 2026 untouched.
would falsify itAttack angle alone predicts as well as the offset does, meaning the pitch's plane adds nothing.
main riskEverything will be significant at 1M swings. Effects get reported in mph and run value, never in p-values.
T2

Every hitter has a different optimal attack angle, set by his bat speed

actionable~450 hitter-seasons

There is no league-wide correct swing plane. Each hitter has an attack angle that maximises his own production per swing, and the optimum is lower for slower bats, because a slower hitter cannot afford to trade contact for loft.

  1. Reliability first: split-half on attack angle within hitter-season, Spearman–Brown corrected. If attack angle is not a stable individual trait, T2 is dead and we learn that in ten minutes. Expect high — swing length was 87% signal.
  2. Per hitter-season with 300+ tracked swings, fit xwOBA-per-swing against a quadratic in attack angle. The vertex is that hitter's optimum.
  3. Shrink each optimum toward the league optimum by its own standard error, using the machinery already built in calibrate.py.
  4. Regress the shrunk optimum on the hitter's mean bat speed. Predicted direction, recorded now: positive.
  5. Deliverable: a per-hitter distance from your own optimum, in degrees and in runs. That is a number a hitting coach can act on, which almost nothing in this project has been.
would falsify itFlat or non-concave curves, or optimum uncorrelated with bat speed once shrunk.
main riskFitting a vertex per hitter on 300 swings is noisy; the shrinkage has to do real work and must be shown to.
T3

"Staying back on the breaking ball" is measurable, and adjusting is the skill

novel1.0M swings

intercept_ball_minus_batter_pos_y_inches is the depth of contact relative to the hitter's own body. The optimal depth differs by pitch type — out front on offspeed, deeper on fastballs — and hitters who vary their contact depth by pitch type outperform hitters with a fixed contact point, holding overall quality constant.

Why this one is interesting. "He stays back well" is one of the oldest scouting phrases in the sport and has never had a number attached to it. This column attaches one.
  1. Sanity-check sign and units against a known truth: changeups must be contacted further out front than four-seamers. If that does not reproduce, stop and read the field definition.
  2. League baseline: mean and spread of contact depth by pitch-type family × count.
  3. Outcome curves: xwOBA per swing against contact depth, computed separately within each pitch-type family, within hitter.
  4. Define adjustability = a hitter's mean depth on offspeed minus his mean depth on fastballs, shrunk by its standard error.
  5. Split-half adjustability to confirm it is a trait and not a residue.
  6. Test whether adjustability predicts offspeed xwOBA controlling for overall hitting quality. Then the causal version: within hitter across seasons, does his offspeed production move with his own adjustability?
would falsify itAdjustability is unreliable, or reliable but unrelated to offspeed performance.
main riskDepth is mechanically tied to pitch velocity, so the fastball/offspeed gap may be arithmetic rather than skill. Control on velocity, not just pitch name.
T4

Pull rate is geometry, not intent — and that rescues it as a metric

highest value486k batted balls

Spray angle should be close to determined by attack direction, contact depth and pitch location. If it is, "he is trying to pull the ball more" is a description of timing, not a decision — and the right way to measure a hitter's pull tendency is through the swing that causes it rather than the outcomes that result.

This is the direct answer to your objection. Pull rate measured as an outcome was 29% signal year to year — the noisy kind of batted-ball stat I was dismissing. But attack_direction is measured on every swing, not just the ~450 that become batted balls, so it should carry several times the sample and far more signal. If pull rate is geometry, we can rescue a batted-ball metric by measuring its cause instead of its effect. That pattern, if it works here, generalises to launch angle and to ground-ball rate.
  1. Compute spray angle from hc_x/hc_y, handedness-corrected. The formula already exists in team_changes.py and has been validated.
  2. Within hitter, regress spray angle on attack direction, contact depth, plate location, pitch type and pitch velocity. Report R². Above roughly 0.6 and spray is mechanically determined.
  3. Split-half reliability of attack_direction versus split-half reliability of observed pull rate, on identical hitter-season samples. Predicted: attack direction is dramatically more reliable.
  4. Re-run the season-over-season signal-share calculation using attack direction in place of pull rate, and compare directly against the 29% baseline.
  5. Take the residual — pull tendency after geometry is removed — and ask whether it is itself a repeatable skill or noise.
would falsify itLow R², meaning spray angle is driven by something not in the swing-geometry columns.
main riskContact depth and attack direction may be near-collinear, which would inflate R² without identifying either. Check the condition number.
Phase B · contact → outcome

What happens to the ball once it is struck

Where the run value actually lands. The point of this phase is one reusable output: a batted-ball residual that is genuinely luck, which every other study can then subtract.

T5

xwOBA leaves money on the table, and we can say exactly how much

infrastructure486k batted balls

estimated_woba_using_speedangle uses exit velocity and launch angle and deliberately nothing else. Adding spray angle, park, fielding alignment and batter speed should improve out-of-sample prediction of what actually happened, materially.

Not novel, but load-bearing. This is roughly what THE BAT X and Baseball Prospectus's DRC+ do commercially. The exclusion of spray angle from xwOBA is a deliberate design choice by MLB — they wanted a metric describing what the hitter controls, not what happened — so this is not a flaw being corrected, it is a different question being asked.
  1. Baseline: actual woba_value predicted by the existing xwOBA. Record out-of-sample R² on 2026. Out of sample from the first line — the earlier gap regression reported in-sample R² and that was a mistake I do not want to repeat.
  2. Model 2 adds spray angle and its interaction with handedness.
  3. Model 3 adds park, infield alignment and outfield alignment.
  4. Model 4 adds a batter speed proxy. The sprint_speed table has 552 rows — one season — so either backfill it or fall back on age_bat plus infield-hit rate as a proxy, and say which.
  5. Fit on 2024–2025, test on 2026. Gradient boosting and a spline GLM, both, so the answer is not an artifact of one functional form.
  6. Decompose the improvement by variable, so the output is "spray angle is worth X points of R², park is worth Y" rather than one number.
  7. Ship the residual as a column other studies can use.
would falsify itOut-of-sample gain under about 0.02 R², meaning EV and LA already carry nearly everything.
main riskPark and alignment partly encode who is hitting. The holdout protects against overfitting but not against that confound.
T6

The shift ban, and the 2022 problem

needs a decisionsee below

The 2023 infield-shift restriction should have helped left-handed pull hitters most. But 35% of batted balls are still non-Standard alignment, so the question is how much of the shift's value teams have quietly recovered through "Infield shade".

this is the one that needs your input

The backfill starts in 2023, which is already post-ban. There is no before in this database, so the natural experiment cannot be run as written. Two honest options: (a) extend the backfill to 2021–2022 — roughly 1.4 million more pitches and 250,000 more batted balls, the code already exists and it is a few hours of unattended download; or (b) drop the before/after and ask only the within-era question: on identical batted balls, does alignment still change the outcome? That is answerable today and is arguably the more useful question anyway, since no one can bring the shift back.

  1. Option (b) version: bucket batted balls by exit velocity, launch angle and spray angle, and compare outcomes across Standard / Infield shade / Strategic within bucket.
  2. Split by handedness. The prediction on record: alignment matters most for left-handed pulled ground balls and essentially not at all for anything in the air.
  3. Convert to runs per 600 plate appearances so the number means something.
  4. If option (a) is approved, add the 2021–2022 seasons and run the full difference-in-differences with right-handed hitters as the control group.
would falsify itNo outcome difference across alignments within matched batted-ball buckets.
main riskAlignment is chosen in response to the hitter, so it is not random. Matching on the batted ball helps but does not fully close it.
T7

Park factors, done as "the same ball in a different yard"

cheap486k batted balls

A park factor should mean: this exact batted ball, hit in that park instead. Traditional park factors compare team run totals and therefore confound the park with the roster that plays in it half the time.

  1. Predict outcome from exit velocity, launch angle and spray angle, pooled across the league.
  2. Each park's mean residual is its effect, measured on physically identical batted balls.
  3. Split by spray-angle bucket to recover the shape — Fenway's left field and Yankee Stadium's right field should appear as opposite-signed bumps in different spray bands, and if they do not, the method is broken.
  4. Feed the result back into T5 as a park adjustment, and separately into yesterday's finding that bat speed itself is park-dependent (sd 0.23 mph, correlated +0.46 with the exit-velocity park effect). Physics should move exit velocity and not bat speed; the ratio separates the ballpark from the instrument.
would falsify itPark residuals with no spatial structure, i.e. noise rather than geometry.
main riskAltitude and temperature vary within park by date; Coors is the control that separates air density from wall distance.
Phase C · across the chain

Who controls contact, and when it changes

Cheaper, more conventional, and each one is a few hours. Worth running as a block once the infrastructure from A and B exists.

T8

Consistency of launch angle beats average launch angle

moderate

A hitter averaging 12° with a spread of 12° is better than one averaging 12° with a spread of 20°. Tightness, not centre, is the skill.

  1. First establish that the spread is even measurable: split-half reliability of a hitter's launch-angle standard deviation. If it is not reliable there is nothing to study.
  2. If reliable, test whether it predicts wOBA controlling for mean launch angle and exit velocity.
  3. Then connect it upstream: does launch-angle spread track swing_path_tilt consistency from Phase A? That would turn an outcome statistic into a mechanical one.
would falsify itSpread is unreliable, or adds nothing over the mean.
main riskLaunch-angle spread is mechanically tied to batted-ball-type mix, so it may just be a restatement of ground-ball rate.
T9

What the two-strike approach actually costs, in runs

quick

We established yesterday that 89% of hitters slow their bat with two strikes. This prices it: how much worse is the batted ball that results, and therefore what is avoiding two strikes worth?

  1. Within hitter, compare exit velocity, launch angle and xwOBA on contact with two strikes versus without.
  2. Control for pitch quality — pitchers throw differently with two strikes, so the raw gap is partly the pitch, not the swing.
  3. Combine with the contact-rate gain to get a net run value of the adjustment, per hitter.
  4. Cross-check against T2: hitters whose two-strike attack angle moves closer to their own optimum should gain most.
would falsify itNo batted-ball penalty once pitch quality is controlled.
main riskTwo-strike pitches are genuinely harder; the control has to be at the pitch level, not the count level.
T10

Aging shows up in exit velocity years before it shows up in results

quick

Peak exit-velocity age is earlier than peak wOBA age, because plate discipline improves with age and masks the physical decline for several seasons.

  1. Delta method: year-over-year changes within player, bucketed by age. This avoids the survivor bias that ruins naive age curves — bad old players retire, so cross-sectional curves make aging look gentler than it is.
  2. Build the curve separately for exit velocity, bat speed, chase rate, walk rate and wOBA.
  3. Compare peak ages. Predicted, recorded now: bat speed and exit velocity peak earliest, walk rate latest, wOBA in between.
would falsify itAll curves peak at the same age.
main riskFour seasons is a short window for an age curve; each player contributes at most three deltas.
T11

Who controls contact quality: the pitcher, the hitter, or the location

moderate

A variance decomposition of batted-ball quality into the pitch's contribution (type, velocity, movement, location), the hitter's, and the interaction. Prediction on record: location dominates both identities.

  1. Fit xwOBA-on-contact with crossed random effects for batter and pitcher plus fixed effects for pitch characteristics and location bucket.
  2. Report the variance share of each component.
  3. Sanity check against the known result that pitchers have far less control over batted-ball outcomes than hitters do — the foundational finding behind FIP. If this does not reproduce that, the model is wrong, not the literature.
would falsify itNothing, strictly — this is a measurement rather than a hypothesis, so it must be framed as one.
main riskCrossed random effects on 486k rows is the one computationally heavy item here.
standing rules

What keeps this honest

  • 2026 is the holdout, and it is not touched during development. Everything is fit on 2024–2025 and evaluated once on 2026. Yesterday every result was fit and read on the same seasons, which is the single biggest weakness of that work.
  • No p-values as headline results. At 486,000 batted balls and 1.0 million swings, statistical significance carries no information. Every finding is reported in mph, degrees, or runs per 600 plate appearances, alongside the league true-change spread from calibrate.py.
  • Every study begins with a positive control. It must first recover something already known — barrels are good, higher exit velocity is better, pitchers control batted balls less than hitters do. A study that cannot reproduce a known truth is a broken pipeline, and finding that out at the end is expensive.
  • Absolute units only. D15 from yesterday: no analysis is measured relative to a provider-derived column that can be redefined between seasons. That rule is what caught the sz_top trap and it applies here to zone and launch_speed_angle.
  • Predicted directions are written down before the query runs — they are in each card above, and they go into RESEARCH_BACKLOG.md before any of this executes. Eleven theories will produce roughly one false positive at conventional thresholds even if every one is false.
  • Phase A is a 2024–2026 program. 2023 swing tracking is 42% populated and will be excluded rather than silently down-weighted.
for approval

Four decisions before anything runs

1. Scope — all three phases, or Phase A first? Phase A is the novel part and the part that answers your objection directly: it rescues batted-ball metrics by measuring the swing that causes them. Phase B builds infrastructure everything else uses. Phase C is conventional and cheap. My recommendation is A first, then B, with C as filler — but running A and B together is defensible since T5's residual feeds T4.
2. Backfill 2021–2022 for the shift ban? Roughly 1.4M more pitches, 250k more batted balls, a few hours unattended. It unlocks a genuine before/after natural experiment, and it also doubles the runway for the aging curves in T10. Against: it is the only item here with a real time cost, and the within-era version of T6 is answerable today.
3. Sprint speed — backfill it or proxy around it? The sprint_speed table has 552 rows, one season. T5 wants batter speed as a predictor and the earlier wOBA-gap study wanted it too. It is a small, separate Savant endpoint. Backfilling four seasons is cheap; proxying with age and infield-hit rate is free but worse.
4. Deliverable shape? Each theory can land as a section in one long report, or each phase can get its own interactive page — Phase A in particular wants an explorer, since "xwOBA per swing against attack-angle offset, filtered by hitter" is a shape you want to poke at rather than read. Default if you have no preference: one written report per phase, plus one interactive explorer for Phase A.
