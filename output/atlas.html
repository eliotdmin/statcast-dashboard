Three results from today

The first two are the strongest candidates this project has produced for a genuinely interesting finding, in the sense of being specific, quantified, mechanistic and checkable.

finding 1

The count matters roughly twice as much as the hitter

.351wOBA swing from the worst count to the best
.185wOBA swing from the worst everyday hitter to the best, over four seasons
1.89×ratio

Reaching an 0–2 count drops a plate appearance to .2074; reaching 3–0 lifts it to .5580. Across 286 hitters with 1,000+ plate appearances the worst is .2695 and the best .4546. So an average hitter at 3–0 is better than the best hitter in baseball, and the same hitter at 0–2 is worse than the worst. On standard deviations rather than ranges the gap is wider still: .0992 across counts against .0247 across hitters, a factor of four.

finding 2

The wOBA–xwOBA gap is mostly reached-on-error

xwOBA has been described here for days as mysteriously favouring fast contact hitters. The mechanism turns out to be specific and mostly not about speed. Measured per batted ball, 2023–2026:

Ground balls are where the gap lives. Removing one event class collapses it:

ground ballsngapshare of gap
all203,667+.0234—
excluding reached-on-error200,037+.0113errors = 52%
also excluding fielder's choice197,336+.0071the two = 70%

xwOBA structurally cannot see an error. It models the batted ball — a routine grounder, expected wOBA .218 — while actual wOBA credits the batter .900 for reaching. There are 4,081 such plate appearances, 88.9% of them ground balls, each carrying a gap of +.682. Fielder's choice does the same thing for another 1,503.

Speed is real but secondary. Splitting ground balls by an independent speed marker (triples per batted ball, which is not endogenous to infield hits the way infield-hit rate would be), the gap runs +.0257 for fast hitters against +.0208 for slow — a five-point difference, against the twenty-three-point structural effect.

This chains all the way back. It explains why exit velocity carried a negative coefficient in every gap regression: high-EV hitters hit fewer ground balls, so they collect less of the structural bonus. It explains the persistent over-performers (Altuve, Friedl, Turner, Rafaela — ground-ball contact hitters who are also fast, both effects aligned) and the under-performers (Soto, Guerrero, Perez — slow air-ball hitters). And it explains why the gap persists across seasons at r ≈ +0.29 for hitters: batted-ball profile is a stable trait.

finding 3

And the same gap is pure noise for pitchers

Run identically on 1,335 pitcher-seasons, the gap has split-half reliability 0.213 and season-to-season correlations of +0.104, −0.021, +0.161 — centred on nothing. For hitters the same three-year-pair test gives +0.293, +0.303, +0.274.

Which follows directly from finding 2: the persistent part of the hitter gap comes from batted-ball profile and foot speed, and a pitcher has neither. He does not choose his launch angles and he does not run to first base. This is a clean, mechanistic prediction that the data confirms, and it is the sort of asymmetry that makes the explanation credible rather than merely fitted.

The thing that is not in this table

The gap column barely moves: every count sits between +.0047 and +.0085, all positive. That is not a count effect — it is the league-wide calibration offset explained in the gap tab, showing up identically everywhere. Which is itself worth knowing: a five-point wOBA-minus-xwOBA reading is inside the metric's own baseline offset, in any count, for any hitter.

What explains wOBA minus xwOBA

Four days of work converged here. The answer has three layers, and the first two were wrong before they were right.

layer 1 · retracted

"Ground-ball singles explain it" — circular

The first regression found ground-ball single rate dominant at R² = .246. A ground ball that finds a hole is both a ground-ball single and a positive gap: the same event on both sides. Its own split-half reliability is .193. Splitting predictors into knowable before the ball lands versus derived from the outcome gives the honest figure: .034 at one month, .137 at three.

layer 2 · correct but incomplete

It is not a within-season skill, but it persists across seasons

Yet across seasons it correlates with itself at +0.293, +0.303, +0.274 over three independent year-pairs. Those two facts were in tension for two days.

layer 3 · the mechanism

It is a structural property of what xwOBA can see

xwOBA is a function of exit velocity and launch angle. It therefore cannot represent three things that decide what a ground ball becomes: where it was hit, how fast the batter runs, and whether a fielder mishandled it. The third is the largest.

+.023xwOBA's error on a ground ball
52%of that is reached-on-error alone
70%adding fielder's choice
−.015xwOBA's error on a popup, the other direction

The tension in layer 2 resolves cleanly. Within a season the gap looks like noise because on any given ground ball an error is a coin flip. Across seasons it persists because ground-ball rate is a stable trait (split-half .783) and a hitter who puts more balls on the ground collects more of a structural bonus that xwOBA can never award him.

What this licenses you to say

  • Not "he is unlucky" for a hitter under-performing xwOBA by five points. That is inside the league-wide calibration offset (+.004 to +.013 depending on season).
  • Yes "xwOBA systematically under-rates ground-ball hitters and over-rates popup-prone ones, by about twenty-three and fifteen points per batted ball respectively."
  • Yes "a hitter's persistent gap is mostly his batted-ball profile, not his luck" — and it is predictable from ground-ball rate before the season starts.
  • Not the same sentence about a pitcher. For pitchers the gap has no persistence at all.

Pitchers, and the xERA question

xERA is not in this archive. The expected_stats table carries est_ba, est_slg and est_woba only — no ERA of any kind. True ERA is also not reconstructible from the pitch data, because earned versus unearned is an official scorer's judgement that is not recorded here.

What is available is the same structure one layer down, and arguably cleaner: wOBA-against versus xwOBA-against, which is what Savant's xERA is derived from anyway (it is a rescaling of expected wOBA onto the ERA scale), plus actual runs allowed computed from the score columns. ERA adds nothing to that except bullpen inheritance, official scoring, and a less convenient scale.

Expected beats realised by more for pitchers than for hitters

Two things stand out. xwOBA-against beats wOBA-against by 34% in relative terms, a much larger edge than the hitter version showed at season level (0.127 vs 0.119). And strikeout and walk rates alone reach 0.156 — nearly matching expected wOBA, using no batted-ball data whatsoever. That is the DIPS result from 1999 reproduced with modern data: what a pitcher controls is mostly the outcomes that never become a batted ball.

How much of run prevention is contact quality?

Correlation with actual runs allowed per nine innings, 1,335 pitcher-seasons: wOBA-against +0.776, xwOBA-against +0.595. wOBA-against should win by construction — it contains the hits that scored the runs. The margin between them is the combined sequencing, defence and timing term, and it is substantial: roughly a third of the explained variance in runs allowed is not contact quality.

What is still open

Ordered by what would change the most if answered.

  1. Steamer and ZiPS. Marcel is the floor of the field, not its standard. Blocked on a manual export: FanGraphs gates CSV behind membership, and the live page serves the continuously updated variant which already contains 2026 — using it would reproduce the leak already caught once. The pre-season files (type=steamer, not steameru) are what is needed.
  2. Confirm the error mechanism on a holdout. Today's finding is measured on all four seasons at once. The clean version predicts a hitter's next-season gap from his prior-season ground-ball rate alone, out of sample. If that works it is a usable projection adjustment, not just an explanation.
  3. Is the season-level model failure sample size? A 20-feature model at R² = −0.060 on 267 training seasons is textbook overfitting, but that is a story, not a test. Backfilling 2015–2022 quintuples the training set — though bat tracking cannot follow, since it does not exist before 2024.
  4. Replicate the 22–25 age result prospectively. Eight segments were tested; one interval excluding zero is about what chance produces.
  5. The count table invites a real question. If the count swings production twice as much as hitter identity, the value of a pitch is mostly its effect on the count. That reframes pitch selection as count management and is directly testable against the sequencing work already done.
  6. Build the shrinkage estimator rather than the model. A shrunk average of past xwOBA ties Marcel exactly. If the value is in the shrinking, the deliverable is two numbers, not a fitted model.
  7. Pitchers beyond the gap. Everything in the Marcel benchmark is hitters. The pitcher prior is a lower ceiling and a larger expected-statistic edge, which the season-level result here already hints at.
  8. Resolve the reliability tension formally. A statistic with within-season reliability .164 that correlates +0.29 with itself across seasons deserves a variance-components model with player, season and residual terms rather than an argument.
